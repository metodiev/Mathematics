# Analytic Geometry and Conic Sections — Solutions

> [Tutorial](README.md) · [Exercises](exercises.md) · 36 worked solutions

Each solution states the strategy, carries out every significant step, and checks the result against the
definition or at a known point. Elements are listed in a consistent order: centre or vertex, axes, foci,
directrices, asymptotes, eccentricity.

## Beginner

### Solution 1

**Standard form.** Divide $y=5x^2$ by $5$: $x^2=\frac15y$, so $4p=\frac15$.

**Elements.** $p=\frac{1}{20}=0.05$; focus $\left(0,\frac1{20}\right)$; directrix $y=-\frac1{20}$; vertex
$(0,0)$; latus rectum $4p=\frac15$.

*Check.* The focus and the vertex are both on the $y$-axis, and the focus is inside the opening (above the
vertex), as it must be for $p>0$. Also $p=\frac{1}{4a}=\frac{1}{4\cdot5}$ ✓.

### Solution 2

**Standard form.** $y^2=12x$ is already of the form $y^2=4px$ with $4p=12$, so $p=3$.

**Elements.** Focus $(3,0)$; directrix $x=-3$; vertex $(0,0)$; axis the $x$-axis; latus rectum
$4p=12$.

*Check.* The point $(3,6)$ is on the parabola since $36=12\cdot3$ ✓. Its distance to the focus $(3,0)$ is
$6$, and its distance to $x=-3$ is $3-(-3)=6$ ✓.

### Solution 3

**Focal distance.** The focus is $(0,3)$, the vertex is the origin, and the opening is upward, so $p=3$.

**Equation and directrix.**
$$x^2=4py=12y,\qquad y=-3 .$$
*Check.* The point $(6,3)$ satisfies $36=12\cdot3$ ✓; its distance to $(0,3)$ is $6$ and to $y=-3$ is $6$
✓.

### Solution 4

**Semi-axes.** $a^2=36$ and $b^2=16$ with the larger denominator under $x$, so $a=6$, $b=4$ and
$$c=\sqrt{a^2-b^2}=\sqrt{36-16}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5\approx4.47 .$$

**Elements.** Centre $(0,0)$; major axis $2a=12$ horizontal; minor axis $2b=8$;
$e=\dfrac{c}{a}=\dfrac{2\sqrt5}{6}=\dfrac{\sqrt5}{3}\approx0.745$; foci $(\pm2\sqrt5,0)$; directrices
$$x=\pm\frac{a^2}{c}=\pm\frac{36}{2\sqrt5}=\pm\frac{18}{\sqrt5}=\pm\frac{18\sqrt5}{5}\approx\pm8.05 .$$

*Check.* At the vertex $(6,0)$ the focal distances are $6-2\sqrt5\approx1.53$ and $6+2\sqrt5\approx10.47$,
summing to $12=2a$ ✓.

### Solution 5

**Orientation.** The larger denominator $25$ is under $y$, so the major axis is vertical: $a=5$, $b=3$,
$$c=\sqrt{25-9}=4,\qquad e=\frac45=0.8 .$$

**Elements.** Foci $(0,\pm4)$; vertices $(0,\pm5)$; co-vertices $(\pm3,0)$; directrices
$y=\pm\frac{25}{4}=\pm6.25$.

*Check.* $c^2+b^2=16+9=25=a^2$ ✓.

### Solution 6

**Semi-axes.** $a^2=49$, $b^2=9$, and for a hyperbola $c^2=a^2+b^2$:
$$a=7,\quad b=3,\quad c=\sqrt{49+9}=\sqrt{58}\approx7.62,\quad e=\frac{\sqrt{58}}{7}\approx1.088 .$$

**Elements.** Vertices $(\pm7,0)$; foci $(\pm\sqrt{58},0)$; asymptotes $y=\pm\frac37x$; transverse axis
horizontal of length $14$; directrices $x=\pm\frac{49}{\sqrt{58}}\approx\pm6.43$; latus rectum
$\frac{2\cdot9}{7}=\frac{18}{7}\approx2.57$.

*Check.* At the vertex $(7,0)$ the distances to the foci are $\sqrt{58}-7\approx0.62$ and
$\sqrt{58}+7\approx14.62$, difference $14=2a$ ✓.

### Solution 7

**Semi-axes.** The positive term is in $y$, so the branches open vertically: $a^2=16$, $b^2=25$,
$$a=4,\quad b=5,\quad c=\sqrt{16+25}=\sqrt{41}\approx6.40,\quad e=\frac{\sqrt{41}}{4}\approx1.601 .$$

**Elements.** Vertices $(0,\pm4)$; foci $(0,\pm\sqrt{41})$; asymptotes $y=\pm\frac abx=\pm\frac45x$;
directrices $y=\pm\frac{16}{\sqrt{41}}\approx\pm2.50$.

*Check.* The asymptote slope is $\frac{a}{b}$, not $\frac{b}{a}$, because the transverse axis is vertical
✓; and $a^2+b^2=16+25=41=c^2$ ✓.

### Solution 8

**Standard form.** Divide by $36$:
$$\frac{4x^2}{36}+\frac{9y^2}{36}=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1 .$$

**Elements.** $a=3$, $b=2$, $c=\sqrt{9-4}=\sqrt5\approx2.24$;
$e=\frac{\sqrt5}{3}\approx0.745$; centre $(0,0)$; foci $(\pm\sqrt5,0)$; vertices $(\pm3,0)$; co-vertices
$(0,\pm2)$; directrices $x=\pm\frac{9}{\sqrt5}=\pm\frac{9\sqrt5}{5}\approx\pm4.02$.

*Check.* $a^2=b^2+c^2=4+5=9$ ✓.

### Solution 9

**Focal distance.** The focus is $(0,-2)$ and the vertex is the origin, so the parabola opens downward with
$p=-2$.

**Equation and directrix.**
$$x^2=4py=-8y,\qquad y=2 .$$
*Check.* The point $(4,-2)$ lies on the curve since $16=-8(-2)$ ✓; its distance to $(0,-2)$ is $4$ and to

### Solution 10

**Semi-minor axis.** $b^2=a^2-c^2=100-36=64$, so $b=8$.

**Elements.**
$$\frac{x^2}{100}+\frac{y^2}{64}=1,\qquad e=\frac{6}{10}=0.6 .$$
Foci $(\pm6,0)$; vertices $(\pm10,0)$; co-vertices $(0,\pm8)$; directrices
$x=\pm\frac{100}{6}=\pm\frac{50}{3}\approx\pm16.67$; latus rectum $\frac{2\cdot64}{10}=12.8$.

*Check.* At $(0,8)$ the focal distances are both $\sqrt{36+64}=10$, summing to $20=2a$ ✓.

### Solution 11

**Semi-axes.** The vertices $(\pm3,0)$ give $a=3$ with a horizontal transverse axis. The asymptote slope
is $\frac ba=2$, so $b=2a=6$.

**Equation and related data.**
$$\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{y^2}{36}=1,\qquad c=\sqrt{9+36}=3\sqrt5\approx6.71,\qquad e=\sqrt5\approx2.236 .$$

*Check.* Substituting $y=2x$ into the equation gives
$\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{4x^2}{36}=\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{x^2}{9}=0$, which is the limiting behaviour of an
asymptote ✓.

### Solution 12

**Completing the square.**
$$x^2-6x+y^2+8y=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;(x-3)^2-9+(y+4)^2-16=0
\;\Longrightarrow\;(x-3)^2+(y+4)^2=25 .$$
So the circle has centre $(3,-4)$ and radius $5$.

**Classification.** Here $A=1$, $B=0$, $C=1$, so $\Delta=0-4=-4<0$: an ellipse. Since $A=C$ and $B=0$ it is
a circle, the ellipse of eccentricity $0$.

*Check.* $(0,0)$ is on the original circle: $0+0-0+0=0$ ✓, and its distance to $(3,-4)$ is $5$ ✓.

## Intermediate

### Solution 13

**Complete the square in $y$.**
$$y^2-6y+8x+25=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;(y-3)^2-9+8x+25=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;(y-3)^2=-8x-16=-8(x+2).$$

**Elements.** The equation is $(y-k)^2=4p(x-h)$ with $(h,k)=(-2,3)$ and $4p=-8$, so $p=-2$. The parabola
opens to the left.

* Vertex $(-2,3)$; axis $y=3$ (horizontal); focus $(-2-2,\,3)=(-4,3)$; directrix
  $x=-2-(-2)=0$.

*Check.* The point with $x=-4$ satisfies $(y-3)^2=16$, so $y=7$ or $y=-1$. For $(-4,7)$: distance to the
focus $(-4,3)$ is $4$; distance to the directrix $x=0$ is $4$ ✓.

### Solution 14

**Discriminant.** $A=4$, $B=0$, $C=9$, so $\Delta=-4\cdot4\cdot9=-144<0$: an ellipse.

**Completing the square.**
$$4(x^2-4x)+9(y^2+2y)=11 \;\Longrightarrow\;4\left[(x-2)^2-4\right]+9\left[(y+1)^2-1\right]=11,$$
$$4(x-2)^2+9(y+1)^2=11+16+9=36 \;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{(x-2)^2}{9}+\frac{(y+1)^2}{4}=1 .$$

**Elements.** Centre $(2,-1)$; $a=3$ horizontal, $b=2$, $c=\sqrt{9-4}=\sqrt5\approx2.24$;
$e=\frac{\sqrt5}{3}\approx0.745$; vertices $(5,-1)$ and $(-1,-1)$; co-vertices $(2,1)$ and $(2,-3)$;
foci $(2\pm\sqrt5,-1)$; directrices $x=2\pm\frac{9}{\sqrt5}\approx2\pm4.02$.

*Check.* At the centre's height, $y=-1$, the equation gives $(x-2)^2=9$, so $x=5$ or $-1$ ✓.

### Solution 15

**Discriminant.** $A=4$, $B=0$, $C=-9$, so $\Delta=0-4\cdot4\cdot(-9)=144>0$: a hyperbola.

**Completing the square.**
$$4(x^2+4x)-9(y^2+2y)=29 \;\Longrightarrow\;4\left[(x+2)^2-4\right]-9\left[(y+1)^2-1\right]=29,$$
$$4(x+2)^2-9(y+1)^2=29+16-9=36 \;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{(x+2)^2}{9}-\frac{(y+1)^2}{4}=1 .$$

**Elements.** Centre $(-2,-1)$; $a=3$ horizontal, $b=2$; $c=\sqrt{9+4}=\sqrt{13}\approx3.61$;
$e=\frac{\sqrt{13}}{3}\approx1.202$; vertices $(1,-1)$ and $(-5,-1)$; foci $(-2\pm\sqrt{13},-1)$;
asymptotes $y+1=\pm\frac23(x+2)$; directrices $x=-2\pm\frac{9}{\sqrt{13}}\approx-2\pm2.50$.

*Check.* $c^2=a^2+b^2=9+4=13$ ✓ and $e>1$ ✓.

### Solution 16

**Vertex.** The vertex lies halfway between the focus and the directrix, on the axis (the horizontal line
through the focus). The distance from the focus to the directrix is $1-(-3)=4=2|p|$, so $|p|=2$ and the
vertex is at
$$\left(\frac{1+(-3)}{2},\,1\right)=(-1,1).$$
Since the focus is to the right of the vertex, the parabola opens to the right and $p=2$.

**Equation.** With $(h,k)=(-1,1)$ and horizontal opening,
$$(y-k)^2=4p(x-h) \;\Longrightarrow\;(y-1)^2=8(x+1).$$

*Check.* A point with $x=1$ satisfies $(y-1)^2=16$, so $y=5$ or $y=-3$. For $(1,5)$: distance to the focus
$(1,1)$ is $4$; distance to $x=-3$ is $4$ ✓.

### Solution 17

**Semi-axes.** $c=4$ and $e=\frac ca=\frac23$ give $a=6$. Then
$$b^2=a^2-c^2=36-16=20 .$$

**Equation.**
$$\frac{x^2}{36}+\frac{y^2}{20}=1 .$$

*Check.* $e=\frac{4}{6}=\frac23$ ✓ and $a^2=b^2+c^2=20+16=36$ ✓.

### Solution 18

**Semi-axes.** The foci are on the $y$-axis, so the transverse axis is vertical with $c=13$. From
$e=\frac ca=\frac{13}{5}$ we get $a=5$, and
$$b^2=c^2-a^2=169-25=144 .$$

**Equation.** The positive term belongs to $y$:
$$\frac{y^2}{25}-\frac{x^2}{144}=1 .$$
The asymptotes are $y=\pm\frac abx=\pm\frac{5}{12}x$.

*Check.* $c^2=a^2+b^2=25+144=169$ ✓.

### Solution 19

**Read the coefficients.** $A=2$, $B=-3$, $C=4$. The discriminant is
$$\Delta=B^2-4AC=9-4\cdot2\cdot4=9-32=-23<0 .$$

**Conclusion.** Since $\Delta<0$ the equation is of the elliptic type. It is not degenerate here: completing
the square in $x$ gives a positive right-hand side, so the curve is a genuine ellipse. (No rotation is
needed to decide the *type*; only to find the axes.)

### Solution 20

**Relation.** The latus rectum of an ellipse is $\frac{2b^2}{a}$, so
$$\frac{2b^2}{5}=6 \;\Longrightarrow\;b^2=15 .$$

**Then** $c^2=a^2-b^2=25-15=10$, so $c=\sqrt{10}$ and
$$e=\frac ca=\frac{\sqrt{10}}{5}\approx0.632 .$$

*Check.* $e<1$ ✓, and $a^2=b^2+c^2=15+10=25$ ✓.

### Solution 21

**Complete the square.**
$$x^2+2x+y^2-4y+8=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;(x+1)^2-1+(y-2)^2-4+8=0
\;\Longrightarrow\;(x+1)^2+(y-2)^2=-3 .$$

**Conclusion.** A sum of squares is never negative, so there are no real points. The discriminant is
$\Delta=0-4=-4<0$, and the equation is the **empty** member of the elliptic family — the same
classification sign as an ellipse, but no graph. This is exactly the caveat in Theorem 5.9: the sign of
$\Delta$ gives the *type*, not the existence of real points.

### Solution 22

**Vertex form.**
$$y=x^2-4x+7=(x-2)^2-4+7=(x-2)^2+3 \;\Longrightarrow\;(x-2)^2=y-3 .$$

**Elements.** This is $(x-h)^2=4p(y-k)$ with $(h,k)=(2,3)$ and $4p=1$, so $p=\frac14$:
vertex $(2,3)$; opens upward; focus $\left(2,3+\frac14\right)=\left(2,\frac{13}{4}\right)$; directrix
$y=3-\frac14=\frac{11}{4}$.

*Check.* At $x=2$ the point is the vertex $(2,3)$, at distance $\frac14$ from both the focus and the
directrix ✓.

### Solution 23

**Tangency condition.** Substitute $y=x+k$ into $y^2=8x$:
$$(x+k)^2=8x \;\Longrightarrow\;x^2+(2k-8)x+k^2=0 .$$
A line is tangent exactly when this quadratic has a double root, i.e. its discriminant vanishes:
$$(2k-8)^2-4k^2=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;4k^2-32k+64-4k^2=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;k=2 .$$

**Point of tangency.** With $k=2$ the quadratic is $x^2-4x+4=(x-2)^2=0$, so $x=2$ and $y=x+k=4$: the
point is $(2,4)$.

*Check by the tangent formula.* At $(2,4)$ on $y^2=4px$ with $p=2$,
$$y\,y_0=2p(x+x_0) \;\Longrightarrow\;4y=4(x+2) \;\Longrightarrow\;y=x+2,$$
which is the line found above.

## Advanced

### Solution 26

**Set-up.** Let $F_1=(-c,0)$ and $F_2=(c,0)$ with $a>c>0$, and $P=(x,y)$. The condition is
$$\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2}+\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}=2a .$$
Write $d_2=\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}$, so that $\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2}=2a-d_2$.

**First squaring.**
$$(x+c)^2+y^2=4a^2-4a\,d_2+(x-c)^2+y^2 .$$
The terms $y^2$ cancel, and $(x+c)^2-(x-c)^2=4cx$, so
$$4cx=4a^2-4a\,d_2 \;\Longrightarrow\;a\,d_2=a^2-cx .$$

**Justifying the second squaring.** The right-hand side $a^2-cx$ is positive: from the equation, $d_2\ge0$
gives $a^2-cx\ge0$, and it can only vanish if $d_2=0$, i.e. $P=F_2$, which gives $|F_1F_2|=2c=2a$,
contradicting $a>c$. So both sides are positive and squaring is reversible:
$$a^2\left[(x-c)^2+y^2\right]=\left(a^2-cx\right)^2 .$$

**Expanding.**
$$a^2x^2-2a^2cx+a^2c^2+a^2y^2=a^4-2a^2cx+c^2x^2 .$$
The cross terms $-2a^2cx$ cancel:
$$\left(a^2-c^2\right)x^2+a^2y^2=a^2\left(a^2-c^2\right).$$

**Standard form.** Since $a>c$, $b^2:=a^2-c^2>0$, and dividing by $a^2b^2$ gives
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1,\qquad b^2=a^2-c^2,$$
which is the same as $a^2=b^2+c^2$. Reversing the algebra shows every point of this ellipse satisfies the
original condition, so the two descriptions agree. $\blacksquare$

*Geometric meaning of $a^2=b^2+c^2$:* the distance from a focus to a co-vertex $(0,b)$ is
$\sqrt{c^2+b^2}=a$, so the string from one focus to a co-vertex to the other has length $a+a=2a$ — the
ellipse passes through its co-vertices and the "string" is taut there too.

### Solution 27

**Set-up.** Foci $(\pm c,0)$ with $c>a>0$, and the condition
$$\left|\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2}-\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}\right|=2a .$$
Take the right branch, where the distance to $F_1=(-c,0)$ is the larger:
$$\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2}=2a+\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}.$$

**First squaring.**
$$(x+c)^2+y^2=4a^2+4a\,d_2+(x-c)^2+y^2 \;\Longrightarrow\;4cx=4a^2+4a\,d_2
\;\Longrightarrow\;cx-a^2=a\,d_2 .$$
Here $cx-a^2>0$ on the branch: $x\ge a$ and $c>a$ imply $cx\ge ca>a^2$.

**Second squaring.**
$$c^2x^2-2a^2cx+a^4=a^2\left[(x-c)^2+y^2\right]=a^2x^2-2a^2cx+a^2c^2+a^2y^2 ,$$
so the cross terms cancel and
$$\left(c^2-a^2\right)x^2-a^2y^2=a^2\left(c^2-a^2\right).$$

**Standard form.** Since $c>a$, $b^2:=c^2-a^2>0$, and division by $a^2b^2$ gives
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1,\qquad c^2=a^2+b^2 .$$
The left branch satisfies the same equation because the absolute value in the condition absorbs the sign of
the difference. Reversing the steps verifies the converse. $\blacksquare$

*Contrast with Solution 26:* there $a$ was the largest of $a,b,c$; here $c$ is. That single swap is the
whole difference between the two standard equations and between $e<1$ and $e>1$.

### Solution 28

**Set-up.** Let $P=(x_0,y_0)$ be a point of $y^2=4px$ with $y_0\ne0$, so $x_0=\frac{y_0^2}{4p}$. The focus
is $F=(p,0)$.

**Slopes.** Differentiating $y^2=4px$ gives $2yy'=4p$, so the tangent at $P$ has slope
$$\tan\alpha=\frac{2p}{y_0},$$
where $\alpha$ is the angle the tangent makes with the $x$-axis. The line $PF$ has slope
$$\frac{y_0-0}{x_0-p}=\frac{y_0}{\frac{y_0^2}{4p}-p}=\frac{4py_0}{y_0^2-4p^2} .$$

**Double-angle identity.** With $t=\tan\alpha=\frac{2p}{y_0}$,
$$\tan2\alpha=\frac{2t}{1-t^2}
=\frac{\frac{4p}{y_0}}{1-\frac{4p^2}{y_0^2}}
=\frac{\frac{4p}{y_0}}{\frac{y_0^2-4p^2}{y_0^2}}
=\frac{4py_0}{y_0^2-4p^2},$$
which is exactly the slope of $PF$.

**Conclusion.** The ray through $P$ parallel to the axis has slope $0$ and so makes angle $0$ with the
$x$-axis; the tangent makes angle $\alpha$; the line $PF$ makes angle $2\alpha$. Hence the tangent bisects
the angle between the axis-parallel ray and $PF$. By the law of reflection (angle of incidence equals angle
of reflection), a ray travelling parallel to the axis toward $P$ leaves along $PF$, that is, through the
focus. At $y_0=0$ the tangent is vertical and the result holds by continuity. $\blacksquare$

### Solution 29

**Set-up.** The condition is $\operatorname{dist}(P,F)=2\operatorname{dist}(P,\ell)$ with $F=(2,0)$ and
$\ell:x=1$, that is
$$\sqrt{(x-2)^2+y^2}=2|x-1| .$$

**Squaring.** Both sides are non-negative, so squaring preserves the solution set:
$$(x-2)^2+y^2=4(x-1)^2 \;\Longrightarrow\;x^2-4x+4+y^2=4x^2-8x+4 .$$

**Simplify.** Collect everything on one side:
$$0=3x^2-4x-y^2 \;\Longrightarrow\;3x^2-y^2-4x=0 .$$
Complete the square in $x$:
$$3\left(x^2-\tfrac43x\right)-y^2=0
\;\Longrightarrow\;3\left[\left(x-\tfrac23\right)^2-\tfrac49\right]-y^2=0
\;\Longrightarrow\;3\left(x-\tfrac23\right)^2-y^2=\tfrac43 .$$

**Standard form.** Divide by $\tfrac43$:
$$\frac{\left(x-\tfrac23\right)^2}{\tfrac49}-\frac{y^2}{\tfrac43}=1 .$$

**Elements.** Centre $\left(\tfrac23,0\right)$; $a^2=\tfrac49$ so $a=\tfrac23$; $b^2=\tfrac43$ so
$b=\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$;
$$c^2=a^2+b^2=\tfrac49+\tfrac{12}{9}=\tfrac{16}{9},\qquad c=\tfrac43,\qquad
e=\frac{c}{a}=\frac{4/3}{2/3}=2 .$$
The curve is a hyperbola with a horizontal transverse axis and asymptotes
$y=\pm\frac ba\left(x-\frac23\right)=\pm\sqrt3\left(x-\frac23\right)$.

**Verification of $e=2$.** The computed eccentricity equals the ratio used in the definition ✓.

**Check at a vertex.** Setting $y=0$ gives $3\left(x-\frac23\right)^2=\frac43$, so
$\left(x-\frac23\right)^2=\frac49$ and $x=\frac43$ or $x=0$. At $x=\frac43$: distance to $F=(2,0)$ is
$\frac23$, distance to $x=1$ is $\frac13$, ratio $2$ ✓.

### Solution 30

**Set-up.** Put the focus at the pole $O$, the directrix at $x=d$ with $d>0$, and let $e>0$. For a point
$P$ with polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ (with $x=r\cos\theta$),
$$\operatorname{dist}(P,O)=r,\qquad \operatorname{dist}(P,\ell)=d-r\cos\theta .$$

**Derivation.**
$$r=e\left(d-r\cos\theta\right) \;\Longrightarrow\;r+er\cos\theta=ed
\;\Longrightarrow\;r\left(1+e\cos\theta\right)=ed,$$
so
$$r=\frac{ed}{1+e\cos\theta}=\frac{k}{1+e\cos\theta},\qquad k=ed .$$

**The case $e=\frac35$, $k=\frac{16}{5}$.** Then $ed=\frac{16}{5}$ and $d=\frac{16}{3}$. Since $e<1$ the
curve is an ellipse. For an ellipse the semi-latus rectum satisfies $k=a(1-e^2)$, so
$$a=\frac{k}{1-e^2}=\frac{\frac{16}{5}}{1-\frac{9}{25}}=\frac{\frac{16}{5}}{\frac{16}{25}}=5,\qquad
c=ea=3,\qquad b=\sqrt{a^2-c^2}=\sqrt{25-9}=4 .$$

**Polar equation and apsides.**
$$r=\frac{\frac{16}{5}}{1+\frac35\cos\theta}=\frac{16}{5+3\cos\theta}.$$
At $\theta=0$, $r=\frac{16}{8}=2=a-c$ (perihelion); at $\theta=\pi$, $r=\frac{16}{2}=8=a+c$ (aphelion).
Their sum $10=2a$ ✓. $\blacksquare$

### Solution 31

**Rotation formulas.** From $x=x'\cos\theta-y'\sin\theta$ and $y=x'\sin\theta+y'\cos\theta$, substitution
into $Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2$ gives
$$A'=A\cos^2\theta+B\sin\theta\cos\theta+C\sin^2\theta,$$
$$B'=(C-A)\sin2\theta+B\cos2\theta,$$
$$C'=A\sin^2\theta-B\sin\theta\cos\theta+C\cos^2\theta .$$

**Two invariant combinations.** Adding the first and third,
$$A'+C'=A\left(\cos^2+\sin^2\right)+C\left(\sin^2+\cos^2\right)=A+C .$$
Subtracting,
$$A'-C'=A\left(\cos^2-\sin^2\right)+2B\sin\theta\cos\theta=(A-C)\cos2\theta+B\sin2\theta .$$

**Eliminate $\theta$.** Square and add, using $\cos^2 2\theta+\sin^2 2\theta=1$:
$$\left(A'-C'\right)^2+B'^2=\left[(A-C)\cos2\theta+B\sin2\theta\right]^2
+\left[-(A-C)\sin2\theta+B\cos2\theta\right]^2=(A-C)^2+B^2 .$$
The cross terms cancel because they are $\pm2B(A-C)\sin2\theta\cos2\theta$.

**Conclusion.**
$$B'^2-4A'C'=B'^2-\left(A'+C'\right)^2+\left(A'-C'\right)^2
=B^2+(A-C)^2-(A+C)^2=B^2-4AC .$$
So the discriminant is unchanged by any rotation. $\blacksquare$

### Solution 32

**Discriminant.** Here $A=1$, $B=B$, $C=4$, so
$$\Delta=B^2-4\cdot1\cdot4=B^2-16 .$$

**Classification.**

* $|B|<4$: $\Delta<0$, an **ellipse**. Its semi-axes are found by rotating through the angle
  $\cot2\theta=\frac{1-4}{B}=-\frac3B$; the curve closes because the quadratic form $x^2+Bxy+4y^2$ is
  positive definite.
* $|B|>4$: $\Delta>0$, a **hyperbola**.
* $|B|=4$: $\Delta=0$. Now the left side is a perfect square:
  $$x^2\pm4xy+4y^2=(x\pm2y)^2,$$
  so the equation reads $(x\pm2y)^2=1$, i.e. $x\pm2y=1$ or $x\pm2y=-1$: **two parallel lines**. This is
  the degenerate member of the parabolic family.

**The transition.** As $B\to4^-$ the ellipse becomes longer and thinner (one semi-axis tends to $0$) and in
the limit collapses onto the pair of parallel lines; for $B$ just above $4$ the two lines separate into
the two branches of a very flat hyperbola. The three families meet exactly at $\Delta=0$.
$\blacksquare$

### Solution 33

**Set-up.** Let $P=(x_0,y_0)$ be a point of the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$, with foci
$F_1=(-c,0)$, $F_2=(c,0)$, $e=\frac ca$ and $b^2=a^2(1-e^2)$. From the focal-radius formulas,
$$r_1:=|PF_1|=a+ex_0,\qquad r_2:=|PF_2|=a-ex_0 .$$

**Unit vectors along the focal rays.**
$$\mathbf{u}_1=\frac{1}{r_1}\left(-c-x_0,\,-y_0\right),\qquad
\mathbf{u}_2=\frac{1}{r_2}\left(c-x_0,\,-y_0\right).$$
Their sum $\mathbf{u}_1+\mathbf{u}_2$ points along the internal bisector of the angle $F_1PF_2$: two unit
vectors have a sum along the bisector of the angle between them.

**Compute the sum.** The first component is
$$\frac{(-c-x_0)r_2+(c-x_0)r_1}{r_1r_2}.$$
With $c=ea$, expand the numerator:
$$(-ea-x_0)(a-ex_0)+(ea-x_0)(a+ex_0)
=\left(-ea^2+e^2ax_0-ax_0+ex_0^2\right)+\left(ea^2+e^2ax_0-ax_0-ex_0^2\right)
=2e^2ax_0-2ax_0=-2ax_0(1-e^2).$$
The second component is
$$-y_0\left(\frac{1}{r_1}+\frac{1}{r_2}\right)=-y_0\cdot\frac{r_1+r_2}{r_1r_2}=-y_0\cdot\frac{2a}{r_1r_2}.$$
So
$$\mathbf{u}_1+\mathbf{u}_2=\frac{-2a}{r_1r_2}\left(x_0(1-e^2),\,y_0\right),$$
which is parallel to $\left(x_0(1-e^2),\,y_0\right)$.

**Compare with the normal.** The normal to the ellipse at $P$ is the gradient of
$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}$, namely
$$\left(\frac{x_0}{a^2},\frac{y_0}{b^2}\right)=\left(\frac{x_0}{a^2},\frac{y_0}{a^2(1-e^2)}\right)
\parallel\left(x_0(1-e^2),\,y_0\right).$$

**Conclusion.** The normal is parallel to the internal bisector $\mathbf{u}_1+\mathbf{u}_2$, so it bisects
$\angle F_1PF_2$. The tangent, being perpendicular to the normal, makes equal angles with the two focal
rays on the other side. Hence a ray travelling from $F_1$ to $P$ reflects along $PF_2$ and passes through
$F_2$: every ray from one focus reaches the other. $\blacksquare$

### Solution 34

**Construction.** Let the cone have apex $S$, axis $a$ and half-angle $\alpha$, and let the plane $\Pi$
cut one nappe in a closed curve, so $\Pi$ is steeper than a generator. Inscribe a sphere $\Sigma_1$ in the
cone on the same side of $\Pi$ as the apex, tangent to $\Pi$ at a point $F_1$ and tangent to the cone along
a circle $C_1$ whose plane is perpendicular to the axis. Inscribe a second sphere $\Sigma_2$ in the same
nappe on the other side of $\Pi$, tangent to $\Pi$ at $F_2$ and to the cone along a circle $C_2$.

**Key equality.** Let $P$ be a point of the section $\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{K}\cap\Pi$, and let $g$ be the
generator of the cone through $P$, meeting $C_1$ at $Q_1$ and $C_2$ at $Q_2$. Then

* $PF_1$ and $PQ_1$ are two tangent segments from $P$ to the same sphere $\Sigma_1$ (one in the plane
  $\Pi$, one along the generator), so $PF_1=PQ_1$;
* similarly $PF_2=PQ_2$.

Tangency from an external point to a sphere has equal length by the symmetry of the sphere about the line
joining the point to its centre.

**Constant sum.** The points $Q_1,Q_2$ both lie on the generator through $P$, and the distance $Q_1Q_2$
measured along a generator is the same for every generator: it is the distance, measured on the cone,
between the two fixed circles $C_1$ and $C_2$. Hence
$$PF_1+PF_2=PQ_1+PQ_2=Q_1Q_2=\text{constant},$$
so $\mathcal{C}$ is an ellipse with foci $F_1,F_2$ (Definition 5.4). The two tangency points $F_1,F_2$,
where the Dandelin spheres touch the cutting plane, are therefore the foci.

**The eccentricity.** The same construction with a plane cutting both nappes, or with one sphere and the
directrix supplied by the plane of $C_1$, gives
$$e=\frac{\cos\beta}{\cos\alpha},$$
where $\beta$ is the angle between $\Pi$ and the axis: the ratio $PF_1/PQ_1$ is the constant ratio of the
distances from $P$ to the focus and to the line $\Pi\cap(\text{plane of }C_1)$, which is the directrix.
$\blacksquare$

### Solution 35

**Set-up.** Let a chord of slope $m\ne0$ have equation $y=mx+b$, meeting $y^2=4px$ at $A=(x_1,y_1)$ and
$B=(x_2,y_2)$.

**Intersection.** Substituting,
$$(mx+b)^2=4px \;\Longrightarrow\;m^2x^2+(2mb-4p)x+b^2=0 .$$
By Vieta's formulas,
$$x_1+x_2=\frac{4p-2mb}{m^2} .$$

**Midpoint ordinate.** Since both points lie on the line,
$$y_1+y_2=m(x_1+x_2)+2b=m\cdot\frac{4p-2mb}{m^2}+2b=\frac{4p-2mb}{m}+2b=\frac{4p}{m}-2b+2b=\frac{4p}{m}.$$
Therefore the midpoint $\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$ has ordinate
$$\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}=\frac{2p}{m},$$
which does not depend on $b$. So the midpoints of **all** chords of slope $m$ lie on the line
$$y=\frac{2p}{m},$$
a line parallel to the axis of the parabola. (Such a line is called a **diameter** of the parabola.)

**The case $m=0$.** A horizontal line $y=c$ meets $y^2=4px$ in the single point $\left(\frac{c^2}{4p},c\right)$,
so there are no horizontal chords. The formula's limit as $m\to\infty$ gives $y=0$: the midpoints of
vertical chords (which exist, between the two points with the same $x$) lie on the axis, as expected from
symmetry. $\blacksquare$

### Solution 36

**(a) Semi-major axis and eccentricity.** The perihelion and aphelion are the two apsidal distances:
$$a(1-e)=0.6,\qquad a(1+e)=5.4 \;\Longrightarrow\;2a=6.0 \;\Longrightarrow\;a=3\ \mathrm{AU}.$$
Then $a(1+e)=5.4$ gives $1+e=1.8$, so
$$e=0.8,\qquad c=ea=2.4\ \mathrm{AU},\qquad b=\sqrt{a^2-c^2}=\sqrt{9-5.76}=\sqrt{3.24}=1.8\ \mathrm{AU}.$$

**(b) Period.** Kepler's third law in astronomical units and years is $T^2=a^3$, so
$$T=a^{3/2}=3^{3/2}=3\sqrt3\approx5.20\ \text{years}.$$
*Check:* $T^2=27$ and $a^3=27$ ✓.

**(c) Polar equation.** With the Sun at the focus,
$$r=\frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos\theta}=\frac{3(1-0.64)}{1+0.8\cos\theta}
=\frac{1.08}{1+0.8\cos\theta}.$$
At $\theta=0$, $r=\frac{1.08}{1.8}=0.6$ AU: that direction is **perihelion**. At $\theta=\pi$,
$r=\frac{1.08}{0.2}=5.4$ AU: that direction is **aphelion**. The orbit is a very elongated ellipse, which
is why the comet spends most of its time far from the Sun and moves fastest at perihelion (Kepler's second
law).
