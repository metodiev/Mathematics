# Measure Theory — Solutions

> [Measure Theory](README.md) · Solutions to the 38 problems in [exercises.md](exercises.md)

Each solution states the strategy first — which definition or theorem is applied, and why its hypotheses hold —
and then carries out every step. Numbers in parentheses refer to the section of the [tutorial](README.md) where
the relevant result is proved. Where a second route exists it is given as a remark, and the likely pitfalls are
flagged in place.

## Beginner

**1.** *Strategy: check the three axioms of Definition 5.1 one at a time. Because $X$ is finite, "countable
union" is the same as "finite union", so only the family's own list needs to be inspected.*

**(a) $\{\varnothing, X\}$.** $X$ is present. The complements are $X^\complement = \varnothing$ and
$\varnothing^\complement = X$, both present. Unions of members are $\varnothing$, $X$, which are present. This is
the **trivial** σ-algebra.

**(b) $\{\varnothing,\{1\},\{2,3\},X\}$.** $X$ is present; the complement of $\{1\}$ is $\{2,3\}$ and conversely,
and $\varnothing^\complement = X$. Every union of members is one of the four sets. This is a σ-algebra: it is the
one generated by the partition $\{1\}\sqcup\{2,3\}$.

**(c) $\{\varnothing,\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\},X\}$.** Axiom 2 fails: $\{1\}^\complement = \{2,3\}$, which is not in the
family. So this is not a σ-algebra, even though it happens to be closed under unions and contains $X$. The
failure is exactly the reason $(X,\mathcal{A})$ is called a *measurable space* rather than a topology: closure
under unions is not enough.

**(d) $\{\varnothing,\{1,2\},X\}$.** Axiom 2 fails: $\{1,2\}^\complement = \{3\} \notin$ the family. Not a
σ-algebra. Completing it would require adding $\{3\}$ and hence $\{1,3\}$, $\{2,3\}$, i.e. all of
$\mathcal{P}(X)$.

**2.** *Strategy: reduce every operation to complements and countable unions, the only operations the axioms
mention.*

**Difference.** For $A, B \in \mathcal{A}$,
$$
A\setminus B = A \cap B^\complement = (A^\complement \cup B)^\complement .
$$
By axiom 2, $A^\complement \in \mathcal{A}$ and $B^\complement \in \mathcal{A}$; by axiom 3,
$A^\complement \cup B \in \mathcal{A}$ (a union of two sets is the countable union $A^\complement \cup B \cup
\varnothing\cup\cdots$); by axiom 2 again, its complement lies in $\mathcal{A}$.

**Symmetric difference.** $A \triangle B = (A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)$ is a union of two members of
$\mathcal{A}$, hence a member.

**Intersections.** $\bigcap_{n\ge1}A_n = \bigl(\bigcup_{n\ge1}A_n^\complement\bigr)^\complement$: each
$A_n^\complement \in \mathcal{A}$ by axiom 2, the union lies in $\mathcal{A}$ by axiom 3, and its complement lies
in $\mathcal{A}$ by axiom 2.

**3.** *Strategy: use the universal property of the generated σ-algebra (Theorem 7.9).*

Since $\mathcal{E}_1\subseteq\mathcal{E}_2\subseteq\sigma(\mathcal{E}_2)$ and $\sigma(\mathcal{E}_2)$ is a
σ-algebra containing $\mathcal{E}_1$, Theorem 7.9.4 gives $\sigma(\mathcal{E}_1)\subseteq\sigma(\mathcal{E}_2)$.

Let $\mathcal{A}_0 = \{\varnothing, A, A^\complement, X\}$. It is a σ-algebra: $X \in \mathcal{A}_0$;
complements are $\varnothing^\complement = X$, $A^\complement$, $A$, $X^\complement = \varnothing$, all in
$\mathcal{A}_0$; and every union of members is a member (the only non-trivial case is
$A\cup A^\complement = X$). It contains $\{A\}$, so $\sigma(\{A\})\subseteq\mathcal{A}_0$ by Theorem 7.9.4.
Conversely any σ-algebra containing $A$ contains $\varnothing$, $A^\complement$ and $X$, hence contains
$\mathcal{A}_0$; so $\mathcal{A}_0\subseteq\sigma(\{A\})$. Therefore $\sigma(\{A\}) = \mathcal{A}_0$.

Note the degenerate cases: if $A = \varnothing$ or $A = X$ then $\mathcal{A}_0 = \{\varnothing, X\}$, and the
displayed formula is still correct because the four listed sets collapse to two.

**4.** *Strategy: exactly the computation in Proposition 7.7, with the two inclusions made explicit.*

**$\mathcal{C}$ is a σ-algebra.** $X\in\mathcal{C}$ since $X^\complement = \varnothing$ is countable. If
$A\in\mathcal{C}$ then either $A$ is countable, in which case $(A^\complement)^\complement = A$ is countable and
$A^\complement\in\mathcal{C}$, or $A^\complement$ is countable, in which case $A^\complement$ is countable and
again $A^\complement\in\mathcal{C}$. Let $A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal{C}$. If every $A_n$ is countable, then
$\bigcup_n A_n$ is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable. Otherwise choose $k$ with
$A_k^\complement$ countable; then
$$
\Bigl(\bigcup_n A_n\Bigr)^\complement = \bigcap_n A_n^\complement \subseteq A_k^\complement
$$
is countable, so the union is co-countable and belongs to $\mathcal{C}$.

**Singletons.** For $x \in X$, the set $\{x\}$ is countable, so $\{x\}\in\mathcal{C}$; hence $\mathcal{C}$ is
strictly larger than the trivial σ-algebra, and in particular countable sets are measurable.

**Strict inclusion.** Take $X = \mathbb{R}$ and $A = [0,\infty)$: $A$ is uncountable and
$A^\complement = (-\infty,0)$ is uncountable, so $A \notin \mathcal{C}$ while $A \in \mathcal{P}(X)$. For a
general uncountable $X$ one argues the same way after splitting $X$ into two uncountable pieces, which is
possible for every uncountable set.

**5.** *Strategy: split $A\cup B$ and $B$ along $A$ and use finite additivity (Proposition 7.12.1).*

Write $A\cup B = A \sqcup (B\setminus A)$ and $B = (A\cap B)\sqcup(B\setminus A)$; both decompositions are
disjoint unions of measurable sets. Finite additivity gives
$$
\mu(A\cup B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B\setminus A), \qquad \mu(B) = \mu(A\cap B)+\mu(B\setminus A).
$$
Substituting the second into the first,
$$
\mu(A\cup B) + \mu(A\cap B) = \mu(A)+\mu(B\setminus A)+\mu(A\cap B) = \mu(A)+\mu(B).
$$
This computation takes place in $[0,\infty]$, where addition is associative and commutative, so the identity
itself needs **no** finiteness hypothesis.

The finiteness hypothesis is needed to *solve* for $\mu(A\cap B)$: writing
$\mu(A\cap B) = \mu(A)+\mu(B)-\mu(A\cup B)$ requires the subtraction of a finite number, and if
$\mu(A\cup B) = \infty$ the right-hand side is the undefined expression $\infty-\infty$ whenever $\mu(A)$ or
$\mu(B)$ is infinite. Example: on $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L},\lambda)$ take $A = (0,\infty)$ and $B = (1,\infty)$.
Then $A\cup B = (0,\infty)$ and $A\cap B = (1,\infty)$, so all four numbers equal $+\infty$ and the displayed
identity reads
$$
\infty+\infty = \infty+\infty,
$$
which is true in $[0,\infty]$; but the rearranged form would read $\infty = \infty+\infty-\infty$, which is
meaningless because $\infty-\infty$ is not defined. The identity survives because addition in $[0,\infty]$ is
associative and commutative and $+\infty$ is an absorbing element; the rearrangement does not, because
subtraction is not available.

**6.** *Strategy: on a finite space a measure is a weight function on points; countable additivity reduces to
finite additivity.*

**Table.** With $\mu(A) = \mathbf{1}_A(1)+2\mathbf{1}_A(2)$:

| $A$ | $\varnothing$ | $\{1\}$ | $\{2\}$ | $\{1,2\}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mu(A)$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |

**$\mu$ is a measure.** $\mu(\varnothing) = 0$. If $A = \bigsqcup_{n\ge1}A_n$ then each $j \in \{1,2\}$ lies in at
most one $A_n$, so
$$
\mu(A) = \sum_{j\in A}w_j = \sum_{n\ge1}\sum_{j\in A_n}w_j = \sum_{n\ge1}\mu(A_n),
$$
where $w_1 = 1$, $w_2 = 2$; only finitely many terms are non-zero, so the series is really a finite sum. (This is
the general fact that a measure on a countable space is determined by its values on singletons.)

**Integrals.** Since $f = 3\mathbf{1}_{\{1\}}+(-1)\mathbf{1}_{\{2\}}$ is a simple function,
$$
\int_X f\,d\mu = 3\mu(\{1\}) + (-1)\mu(\{2\}) = 3\cdot1 - 1\cdot2 = 1 .
$$
For the absolute value, $\lvert f\rvert = 3\mathbf{1}_{\{1\}}+1\mathbf{1}_{\{2\}}$, so
$\int\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu = 3+2 = 5$; directly, $f^+ = 3\mathbf{1}_{\{1\}}$, $f^- = 1\mathbf{1}_{\{2\}}$, and
$\int f = \int f^+-\int f^- = 3-2 = 1$, consistent.

**Layer cake check.** $\lvert f\rvert$ takes the value $3$ on $\{1\}$ and $1$ on $\{2\}$. For $0\le t<1$,
$\{\lvert f\rvert>t\} = \{1,2\}$ with $\mu$-measure $3$; for $1\le t<3$,
$\{\lvert f\rvert>t\} = \{1\}$ with measure $1$; for $t\ge3$ the set is empty. Hence
$$
\int_X\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu = \int_0^1 3\,dt + \int_1^3 1\,dt = 3+2 = 5 .
$$

**7.** *Strategy: replace the $A_n$ by disjoint pieces contained in them.*

Define $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_n = A_n\setminus\bigcup_{k<n}A_k$ for $n\ge2$. Each $B_n$ is measurable (Problem 2), the
$B_n$ are pairwise disjoint, $B_n\subseteq A_n$, and $\bigcup_n B_n = \bigcup_n A_n$. Countable additivity and
monotonicity give
$$
\mu\Bigl(\bigcup_n A_n\Bigr) = \mu\Bigl(\bigcup_n B_n\Bigr) = \sum_{n\ge1}\mu(B_n) \le \sum_{n\ge1}\mu(A_n).
$$

**Strictness.** On $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L},\lambda)$ let $A_n = (0,1/n)$. Then
$\bigcup_{n\ge1}A_n = (0,1)$ has measure $1$, while $\sum_n\lambda(A_n) = \sum_n 1/n = \infty$. The inequality
$1\le\infty$ is strict; the "loss" comes from the massive overlapping of the sets.

**8.** *Strategy: name the property used in each line — subadditivity, difference formula, or countable
additivity on disjoint unions.*

**(a)** The set $S = \{1/n : n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{0\}$ is countable, hence a countable union of singletons, each
of measure $0$ (Proposition 7.28). By countable subadditivity,
$\lambda(S)\le\sum_{x\in S}\lambda(\{x\}) = 0$, so $\lambda(S) = 0$.

**(b)** $\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)$ is countable, so $\lambda(\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)) = 0$ exactly as in (a).

**(c)** By the difference formula (Proposition 7.12.3) with $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\subseteq[0,1]$ and
$\lambda(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]) = 0<\infty$,
$$
\lambda([0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}) = \lambda([0,1])-\lambda(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]) = 1-0 = 1 .
$$

**(d)** The intervals $(2n,2n+1)$, $n\ge1$, are pairwise disjoint, so countable additivity gives
$$
\lambda\Bigl(\bigcup_{n\ge1}(2n,2n+1)\Bigr) = \sum_{n\ge1}\lambda((2n,2n+1)) = \sum_{n\ge1}1 = \infty .
$$
This is a case where a disjoint union of sets of finite measure has infinite measure; no finiteness is assumed in
countable additivity.

**9.** *Strategy: continuity from below (Theorem 7.13), whose only hypothesis is monotonicity of the sequence.*

For $n\ge2$, $A_n = [0,1-1/n]$ has $\lambda(A_n) = 1-1/n$. The sequence increases because
$1-1/n < 1-1/(n+1)$, and
$$
\bigcup_{n\ge2}A_n = [0,1) ,
$$
since every $x\in[0,1)$ satisfies $x\le1-1/n$ for all large $n$, while $1\notin A_n$ for every $n$. Theorem 7.13
gives
$$
\lambda\Bigl(\bigcup_{n\ge2}A_n\Bigr) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda(A_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\Bigl(1-\frac1n\Bigr) = 1 = \lambda([0,1)) .
$$
Continuity from below requires no finiteness hypothesis, in contrast with Theorem 7.14.

**10.** *Strategy: test the hypothesis of Theorem 7.14.*

Each $A_n = [n,\infty)$ has $\lambda(A_n) = \infty$ (it contains $[n,n+1]$, of length $1$, and is unbounded).
The sequence is decreasing and $\bigcap_{n\ge1}[n,\infty) = \varnothing$, since a real number $x$ lies in no
$A_n$ with $n > x$. Hence
$$
\lambda\Bigl(\bigcap_n A_n\Bigr) = 0 \ne \infty = \lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda(A_n).
$$

The failed hypothesis is "$\mu(A_N)<\infty$ for some $N$": here $\mu(A_N) = \infty$ for every $N$. The proof of
Theorem 7.14 (and the general form in Problem 33) works with $C_n = A_1\setminus A_n$ and needs
$\mu(A_1\setminus A_n) = \mu(A_1)-\mu(A_n)$, which requires $\mu(A_1)<\infty$; without it the identity is
$\infty-\infty$. The correct general conclusion in the decreasing case is only
$\mu(A)\le\lim_n\mu(A_n)$.

**11.** *Strategy: compute the Darboux sums for every partition, then compute the Lebesgue integral from the
definition as a simple function.*

**No Riemann integral.** For any partition $P = \{x_0,\dots,x_m\}$ of $[0,1]$ and any interval $I_i =
[x_{i-1},x_i]$ with $x_{i-1}<x_i$, both $\mathbb{Q}$ and its complement are dense, so $I_i$ contains a rational
and an irrational. Hence
$$
m_i = \inf_{I_i}D = 0, \qquad M_i = \sup_{I_i}D = 1,
$$
and therefore $L(D,P) = \sum_i 0\cdot\Delta x_i = 0$ and $U(D,P) = \sum_i 1\cdot\Delta x_i = 1$. Thus
$\underline{\int_0^1}D = 0$ and $\overline{\int_0^1}D = 1$; since they differ, $D\notin\mathcal{R}[0,1]$, and this
holds for **every** partition, so no refinement helps.

**Lebesgue integral.** $D$ is simple:
$$
D = 1\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]}+0\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}} .
$$
Both level sets are Lebesgue measurable ($\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ is countable, hence Borel; its complement in
$[0,1]$ is Borel as well). By Definition 5.12, stage 2,
$$
\int_{[0,1]}D\,d\lambda = 1\cdot\lambda(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]) + 0\cdot\lambda([0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}) = 1\cdot0+0\cdot1 = 0 .
$$
The convention $0\cdot\infty = 0$ is not even needed here, since both measures are finite. The moral: the Lebesgue
integral sees only the *sizes* of level sets, and the level set where $D = 1$ has size zero.

**12.** *Strategy: exhibit each level set as a Borel set, using Proposition 7.31.*

**$f = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}$.** The level sets are
$$
\{f>a\} = \begin{cases}\mathbb{R}, & a<0,\\ \mathbb{Q}, & 0\le a<1,\\ \varnothing, & a\ge1,\end{cases}
$$
and $\mathbb{R},\mathbb{Q},\varnothing$ are all Borel (every countable set is Borel by Example 7.11). By
Proposition 7.31, $f$ is Borel measurable.

**$g(x) = x\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)$.** For $x$ irrational, $g(x) = 0$; for $x = p/q$ rational, $g(x) = x$. Hence
$$
\{g>a\} = \begin{cases}
(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})\cup\bigl(\mathbb{Q}\cap(a,\infty)\bigr), & a<0,\\[2pt]
\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,\infty), & a = 0,\\[2pt]
\mathbb{Q}\cap(a,\infty), & a>0 .
\end{cases}
$$
Each is a union or intersection of a countable set with an open set, hence Borel; so $g$ is measurable. Note that
$g$ is discontinuous everywhere and unbounded on every interval, which shows how little measurability demands.

## Intermediate

**13.** *Strategy: instantiate the formula of Theorem 7.37 with $n=1$ and $n=2$, and remember that on
$f(x) = x\in[0,1]$ the truncation set $\{f\ge n\}$ is empty for $n\ge1$ except at $x=1$.*

For $n=1$: $k$ runs from $1$ to $1\cdot2^1 = 2$, with $A_{1,k} = \{(k-1)/2\le f<k/2\}$; and
$A_1 = \{f\ge1\} = \{1\}$. Hence
$$
\varphi_1 = 0\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[0,1/2)} + \tfrac12\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[1/2,1)} + 1\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{1\}} .
$$
For $n=2$: $k$ runs from $1$ to $2\cdot2^2 = 8$, with $A_{2,k} = \{(k-1)/4\le f<k/4\}$, and
$A_2 = \{f\ge2\} = \varnothing$. Hence
$$
\varphi_2 = \sum_{k=1}^{8}\frac{k-1}{4}\,\mathbf{1}_{[(k-1)/4,\,k/4)} .
$$

**At $x = 0.3$.** Since $0.3\in[0,\tfrac12)$, $\varphi_1(0.3) = 0$; since $0.3\in[\tfrac14,\tfrac12)$,
$\varphi_2(0.3) = \tfrac14$. Thus $\varphi_1\le\varphi_2\le f$ at this point, and the error is
$f(0.3)-\varphi_2(0.3) = 0.3-0.25 = 0.05\le 2^{-2} = 0.25$.

**At $x = 0.8$.** Since $0.8\in[\tfrac12,1)$, $\varphi_1(0.8) = \tfrac12$; since
$0.8\in[\tfrac34,1)$, $\varphi_2(0.8) = \tfrac34$. Again $\varphi_1\le\varphi_2\le f$, and the error is
$0.8-0.75 = 0.05\le0.25$.

**The three claims.** (i) $0\le\varphi_1\le\varphi_2\le f$ holds at both points and, by the general proof of
Theorem 7.37, everywhere. (ii) Monotonicity in $n$: at $0.3$ the values $0,\tfrac14$ increase, at $0.8$ the values
$\tfrac12,\tfrac34$ increase — in general $\varphi_n$ jumps by at most $2^{-n}$ at each stage. (iii) The error
bound $0\le f-\varphi_n\le2^{-n}$ holds whenever $f<n$; here $f\le1<n$ for $n=1$ fails only at $x=1$, where
$f(1) = 1 = \varphi_1(1)$ anyway, and for $n = 2$ the bound is $0.05\le0.25$.

**14.** *Strategy: measurability is tested on a generating family; the "good sets" form a σ-algebra.*

**The good sets.** Let $\mathcal{G} = \{B\subseteq\mathbb{R} : f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{A}\}$. Then
$\mathbb{R}\in\mathcal{G}$ because $f^{-1}(\mathbb{R}) = X\in\mathcal{A}$; if $B\in\mathcal{G}$ then
$f^{-1}(B^\complement) = \bigl(f^{-1}(B)\bigr)^\complement\in\mathcal{A}$, so $B^\complement\in\mathcal{G}$; and if
$B_1,B_2,\dots\in\mathcal{G}$ then
$f^{-1}\bigl(\bigcup_nB_n\bigr) = \bigcup_nf^{-1}(B_n)\in\mathcal{A}$, so $\bigcup_nB_n\in\mathcal{G}$. Hence
$\mathcal{G}$ is a σ-algebra.

**Equivalence.** If $f$ is measurable then every Borel $B$ lies in $\mathcal{G}$, in particular every interval
$(q,\infty)$, so $\{f>q\}\in\mathcal{A}$ for all $q\in\mathbb{Q}$.

Conversely, suppose $\{f>q\}\in\mathcal{A}$ for all $q\in\mathbb{Q}$; by Proposition 7.31 it is enough to prove
this for all $a\in\mathbb{R}$. Given $a$, choose rationals $q_1>q_2>\cdots$ with $q_n\downarrow a$; then
$f(x)>a$ if and only if $f(x)>q_n$ for some $n$, so
$$
\{f>a\} = \bigcup_{n\ge1}\{f>q_n\}\in\mathcal{A}.
$$
By Proposition 7.31, $f$ is measurable. The point of the exercise is that the σ-algebra generated by the
half-lines $(q,\infty)$, $q\in\mathbb{Q}$, is $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ (Proposition 7.10), so checking a
*countable* family suffices.

**15.** *Strategy: write every set as a combination of level sets of $f$ and $g$, which are measurable by
Proposition 7.31.*

**Maximum and minimum.** For $a\in\mathbb{R}$,
$$
\{f\vee g>a\} = \{f>a\}\cup\{g>a\}\in\mathcal{A}, \qquad f\wedge g = -((-f)\vee(-g)),
$$
so both are measurable (`-f` is measurable because $t\mapsto-t$ is continuous, Proposition 7.34).

**Absolute value.** For $a\ge0$, $\{\lvert f\rvert>a\} = \{f>a\}\cup\{f<-a\}\in\mathcal{A}$; for $a<0$ the set is
$X$. Hence $\lvert f\rvert$ is measurable.

**Square.** $f^2 = \varphi\circ f$ with $\varphi(t) = t^2$. The hypothesis of Proposition 7.34 is that the outer
function be **Borel measurable**; $\varphi$ is continuous, hence Borel, so $f^2$ is measurable. (The same
argument gives $\lvert f\rvert^p$ for every $p>0$.)

**Equality set.** $\{f = g\} = \{f-g = 0\} = (f-g)^{-1}(\{0\})$; $f-g$ is measurable (Proposition 7.33) and
$\{0\}$ is Borel, so the set is measurable. Equivalently,
$\{f = g\} = \bigcap_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\bigl(\{f\le q\}\cup\{g\ge q\}\bigr)^{c}$-type manipulations, but the
preimage argument is shorter.

**16.** *Strategy: the sets $I_n = [1/(n+1),1/n)$ are disjoint, so the partial sums are simple and increase
to $f$; monotone convergence then computes the integral.*

**(a)** Put $s_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N}2^{-n}\mathbf{1}_{I_n}$. Each $s_N$ is simple and non-negative, and
$0\le s_1\le s_2\le\cdots\le f$ with $s_N\uparrow f$ (at every $x$ the series has at most one non-zero term). By
monotone convergence and the definition of the integral of a simple function,
$$
\int_{[0,1]}f\,d\lambda = \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N}2^{-n}\lambda(I_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\Bigl(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\Bigr) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{-n}}{n(n+1)} .
$$
Using $\frac{1}{n(n+1)} = \frac1n-\frac1{n+1}$ and the two series
$\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}/n = \ln2$ and
$\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}/(n+1) = 2\sum_{m\ge2}(1/2)^m/m = 2(\ln2-\tfrac12)$,
$$
\int_{[0,1]}f\,d\lambda = \ln 2 - 2\ln 2 + 1 = 1-\ln 2 \approx 0.3069 .
$$

**(b)** Since $f\ge0$ and $\lambda$ is σ-finite, Corollary 7.67 applies. On $[2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n})$ the level set
$\{f>t\}$ equals $\{f\ge2^{-n}\} = \bigcup_{k\le n}I_k = [1/(n+1),1)$, whose measure is $n/(n+1)$. Hence
$$
\int f = \sum_{n\ge1}\bigl(2^{-n}-2^{-(n+1)}\bigr)\frac{n}{n+1} = \sum_{n\ge1}2^{-(n+1)}\Bigl(1-\frac1{n+1}\Bigr) = \frac12-\bigl(\ln2-\tfrac12\bigr) = 1-\ln2,
$$
using $\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-(n+1)} = \tfrac12$ and
$\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-(n+1)}/(n+1) = \sum_{m\ge2}2^{-m}/m = \ln2-\tfrac12$. The two answers agree, as they must.

**17.** *Strategy: compare $f$ with the simple function $a\mathbf{1}_{\{f\ge a\}}$.*

**Markov.** For $a>0$ and every $x$, $a\mathbf{1}_{\{f\ge a\}}(x)\le f(x)$: if $f(x)\ge a$ both sides equal $a$
and $f(x)$; otherwise the left side is $0$. By monotonicity and scaling (Proposition 7.41.1 and 7.41.2),
$$
a\,\mu(\{f\ge a\}) = \int a\mathbf{1}_{\{f\ge a\}}\,d\mu \le \int f\,d\mu,
$$
and dividing by $a>0$ gives the claim.

**Finiteness a.e.** Suppose $\int f\,d\mu<\infty$. By Markov,
$\mu(\{f\ge n\})\le\frac1n\int f\to0$. The sets $\{f\ge n\}$ decrease to
$\{f = \infty\} = \bigcap_n\{f\ge n\}$, and $\mu(\{f\ge1\})\le\int f<\infty$: this is exactly the finiteness
hypothesis of Theorem 7.14 (continuity from above). Therefore
$$
\mu(\{f=\infty\}) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(\{f\ge n\}) = 0 .
$$
Without $\int f<\infty$ the conclusion can fail: $f = \infty$ everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$ with Lebesgue measure has
$\int f = \infty$ and $\{f = \infty\} = \mathbb{R}$.

**18.** *Strategy: monotone convergence applied to the partial sums.*

Let $s_N = \sum_{n=1}^N f_n$. Each $s_N$ is measurable and non-negative, and $s_N\uparrow s := \sum_{n\ge1}f_n$.
By Theorem 7.43,
$$
\int s\,d\mu = \lim_{N\to\infty}\int s_N\,d\mu = \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int f_n\,d\mu = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int f_n\,d\mu,
$$
where the middle equality is finite additivity of the integral (Corollary 7.44, by induction on $N$). No
hypothesis other than measurability and non-negativity is used, and no disjointness is required.

**Deduction.** With $f_n = \mathbf{1}_{A_n}$, $\sum_n\mathbf{1}_{A_n}(x)$ counts how many of the sets $A_n$
contain $x$ (possibly $\infty$), so
$$
\int\Bigl(\sum_{n\ge1}\mathbf{1}_{A_n}\Bigr)d\mu = \sum_{n\ge1}\mu(A_n)
$$
for arbitrary measurable $A_n$ — this is countable subadditivity "with multiplicity".

**19.** *Strategy: make the mass concentrate at a point, so that it disappears in the limit.*

Take $f_n = n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ on $[0,1]$. For $x>0$, $f_n(x) = 0$ as soon as $n>1/x$, and $f_n(0) = 0$; so
$f_n\to0$ pointwise and $\liminf_nf_n = 0$. But
$$
\int_0^1 f_n\,d\lambda = n\cdot\frac1n = 1 \quad\text{for every }n,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad \liminf_n\int f_n = 1 .
$$
Therefore $\int\liminf_nf_n = 0 < 1 = \liminf_n\int f_n$: Fatou's inequality holds strictly.

**No dominating function.** If $g$ were integrable with $f_n\le g$ for all $n$, then for $x\in(0,\frac12]$ and
$n = \lfloor1/(2x)\rfloor$ we have $n\le\frac{1}{2x}<\frac1x$, hence $f_n(x) = n\ge\frac{1}{2x}-1$, so
$g(x)\ge\frac{1}{2x}-1$ on $(0,\frac12]$ and
$$
\int_0^{1/2}g\,d\lambda \ge \int_0^{1/2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2x}-1\Bigr)dx = \infty .
$$
So no integrable domination exists, which is exactly why Theorem 7.50 does not apply.

**20.** *Strategy: find the pointwise limit, then a fixed integrable dominating function.*

**Pointwise limit.** For $x>0$, $n\sin(x/n) = x\cdot\frac{\sin(x/n)}{x/n}\to x$, so
$\frac{n\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\to\frac{1}{1+x^2}$. At $x=0$ define the integrand to be $1$ (its limit); a single
point is null.

**Domination.** Since $\lvert\sin t\rvert\le\lvert t\rvert$ for all real $t$,
$$
\Bigl\lvert\frac{n\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\Bigr\rvert \le \frac{n\cdot\lvert x\rvert/n}{x(1+x^2)} = \frac{1}{1+x^2} =: g(x), \qquad x>0,
$$
and $g$ is integrable:
$\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2} = \lim_{R\to\infty}\arctan R = \frac{\pi}{2}<\infty$.

**Conclusion.** The functions are measurable, converge a.e., and are dominated by the integrable $g$; Theorem 7.50
applies and gives
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^\infty\frac{n\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\,dx = \int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2} = \frac{\pi}{2} .
$$

**21.** *Strategy: apply Lebesgue's criterion (Theorem 7.56) to identify the discontinuity set, then compute the
Lebesgue integral.*

**Discontinuity set.** $T$ is continuous at every irrational point and discontinuous at every rational point: at
$x = p/q$ the value is $1/q$, while points $p'/q'$ with large $q'$ arbitrarily close to $x$ have values
$1/q'\to0$. Hence $D_T = \mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$, which is countable and therefore has $\lambda$-measure $0$
(Proposition 7.28).

**Riemann integrability.** $T$ is bounded ($0\le T\le1$) and $\lambda(D_T) = 0$, so by Theorem 7.56
$T\in\mathcal{R}[0,1]$.

**Lebesgue integral.** By Theorem 7.55 a Riemann integrable function is Lebesgue integrable with the same
integral. Moreover $T = 0$ a.e. (it vanishes off the null set $\mathbb{Q}$), so by Proposition 7.41.3
$$
\int_{[0,1]}T\,d\lambda = \int_{[0,1]}0\,d\lambda = 0 .
$$
Both routes agree, and the Riemann integral is also $0$.

**Contrast with Problem 11.** $T$ and $D = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}$ differ only on the null set
$\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$, but $T$ is Riemann integrable while $D$ is not: Riemann integrability is not invariant
under changes on null sets, because the Riemann integral cannot ignore a dense set. The Lebesgue integral of both
is $0$, and it *is* invariant under such changes.

**22.** *Strategy: truncate at height $N$ to get bounded functions, apply monotone convergence, and evaluate the
Riemann integrals.*

**Integrability.** Put $f_N = \min(x^{-1/2},N)$ on $(0,1]$. Each $f_N$ is bounded, Riemann integrable on
$[\varepsilon,1]$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, and
$$
\int_0^1 f_N(x)\,dx = \int_0^{N^{-2}}N\,dx+\int_{N^{-2}}^{1}x^{-1/2}dx = N\cdot N^{-2}+\bigl[2x^{1/2}\bigr]_{N^{-2}}^{1} = \frac1N+2-\frac{2}{N} = 2-\frac1N .
$$
(The first piece is where $x^{-1/2}\ge N$, i.e. $x\le N^{-2}$.) Also $f_N\uparrow x^{-1/2}$ pointwise. By
monotone convergence (Theorem 7.43) and Theorem 7.55 (Riemann integrals of bounded functions are Lebesgue
integrals),
$$
\int_{(0,1]}x^{-1/2}d\lambda = \lim_{N\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_N\,dx = \lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigl(2-\frac1N\Bigr) = 2<\infty .
$$
So $x^{-1/2}\in L^1((0,1])$, with integral $2$.

**A shorter route, truncating the domain.** Let $g_\varepsilon = x^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}_{[\varepsilon,1]}$ for $0<\varepsilon<1$. Each $g_\varepsilon$ is bounded and Riemann integrable with
$\int_0^1g_\varepsilon\,dx = \bigl[2x^{1/2}\bigr]_\varepsilon^1 = 2-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, and
$g_\varepsilon\uparrow x^{-1/2}$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Monotone convergence therefore gives
$$
\int_{(0,1]}x^{-1/2}d\lambda = \lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\bigl(2-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}\bigr) = 2 .
$$
Both routes are instances of the same idea: exhaust the unbounded region by bounded pieces and apply Theorem 7.43.

**Not Riemann integrable.** A Riemann integrable function on $[0,1]$ must be bounded (a Riemann sum with a tag
near $0$ can be made arbitrarily large). Since $x^{-1/2}\to\infty$ as $x\downarrow0$, the function is unbounded
and hence not Riemann integrable in the proper sense; the number $2$ above is the value of an *improper* Riemann
integral. This is Example 7.57 in action.

**23.** *Strategy: use Markov's inequality for one direction and the fact that a null set contributes nothing
for the other.*

**From zero integral to zero a.e.** Let $f$ be measurable with $\int\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu = 0$. For every $n$,
Markov's inequality (Proposition 7.41.4, applied to $\lvert f\rvert$) gives
$$
\mu(\{\lvert f\rvert\ge 1/n\}) \le n\int\lvert f\rvert\,d\mu = 0 .
$$
Since $\{\lvert f\rvert>0\} = \bigcup_n\{\lvert f\rvert\ge1/n\}$ is a countable union of null sets, it is null by
subadditivity. So $f = 0$ a.e.

**From zero a.e. to zero integral.** If $f = 0$ a.e. then $\lvert f\rvert = 0$ a.e., and every simple
$0\le\varphi\le\lvert f\rvert$ vanishes a.e., so $\int\varphi = 0$ and hence $\int\lvert f\rvert = 0$
(Proposition 7.41.3).

**Deduction.** If $f = g$ a.e. and $f\in L^1$, then $\lvert f-g\rvert = 0$ a.e., so $\int\lvert f-g\rvert = 0$;
moreover $\lvert g\rvert\le\lvert f\rvert+\lvert f-g\rvert$ gives $\int\lvert g\rvert\le\int\lvert f\rvert<\infty$, so $g$ is integrable. By
linearity (Theorem 7.46),
$$
\int g-\int f = \int(g-f) = 0,
$$
because $\lvert g-f\rvert = 0$ a.e. implies $\int\lvert g-f\rvert = 0$, and $\bigl\lvert\int(g-f)\bigr\rvert\le\int\lvert g-f\rvert$.

**24.** *Strategy: Hölder's inequality with the conjugate pair $(q/p, q/(q-p))$.*

**(a)** Let $f\in L^q$ and $1\le p<q<\infty$. Apply Hölder (Theorem 7.60) to $\lvert f\rvert^p$ and the constant
function $1$, with exponents $q/p$ and $\frac{q}{q-p}$, which are conjugate since
$\frac{p}{q}+\frac{q-p}{q} = 1$:
$$
\int\lvert f\rvert^p\,d\mu \le \Bigl(\int\lvert f\rvert^q\,d\mu\Bigr)^{p/q}\Bigl(\int 1\,d\mu\Bigr)^{(q-p)/q} = \lVert f\rVert_q^p\,\mu(X)^{1-p/q}.
$$
Taking $p$-th roots, $\lVert f\rVert_p\le\mu(X)^{1/p-1/q}\lVert f\rVert_q<\infty$, so $f\in L^p$. For
$q = \infty$, $\lvert f\rvert\le\lVert f\rVert_\infty$ a.e. gives
$\int\lvert f\rvert^p\le\lVert f\rVert_\infty^p\mu(X)$.

**(b) Strict inclusion on $[0,1]$.** Take $f(x) = x^{-1/2}$. By Problem 22, $f\in L^1(0,1)$; but
$$
\int_0^1\lvert f\rvert^2dx = \int_0^1\frac{dx}{x} = \infty,
$$
so $f\notin L^2(0,1)$. Hence $L^2([0,1])\subsetneq L^1([0,1])$.

**Failure for infinite measure.** On $(\mathbb{R},\lambda)$ the function $f = (1+x)^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}$
satisfies
$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{(1+x)^2} = \frac12<\infty, \qquad \int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x} = \infty,
$$
so $f\in L^2$ but $f\notin L^1$: the inclusion is reversed. And $g(x) = x^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}$ lies in $L^1$
but not in $L^2$. So on an infinite measure space there is no inclusion in either direction.

**25.** *Strategy: replay Steps 2 and 3 of the proof of Theorem 7.20, making the algebra explicit.*

**(a) Finite unions.** Let $\mu^*$ be an outer measure and $E,F\in\mathcal{M}$. For any $A\subseteq X$, first
apply the measurability of $E$ to the test set $A\cap E^\complement$:
$$
\mu^*(A\cap E^\complement) = \mu^*(A\cap E^\complement\cap F)+\mu^*(A\cap E^\complement\cap F^\complement).
$$
Next, by subadditivity applied to $A\cap(E\cup F) = (A\cap E)\cup(A\cap E^\complement\cap F)$,
$$
\mu^*(A\cap(E\cup F)) \le \mu^*(A\cap E)+\mu^*(A\cap E^\complement\cap F).
$$
Adding $\mu^*(A\setminus(E\cup F)) = \mu^*(A\cap E^\complement\cap F^\complement)$ and inserting the first display,
$$
\mu^*(A\cap(E\cup F))+\mu^*(A\setminus(E\cup F)) \le \mu^*(A\cap E)+\mu^*(A\cap E^\complement) = \mu^*(A),
$$
the last equality being $E\in\mathcal{M}$. Together with the reverse inequality (subadditivity) this gives
$E\cup F\in\mathcal{M}$. By induction, $\mathcal{M}$ is closed under finite unions.

**(b) Additivity.** If $E,F\in\mathcal{M}$ are disjoint, apply the measurability of $E$ to the test set
$A\cap(E\cup F)$: then $A\cap(E\cup F)\cap E = A\cap E$ and $A\cap(E\cup F)\cap E^\complement = A\cap F$, so
$$
\mu^*(A\cap(E\cup F)) = \mu^*(A\cap E)+\mu^*(A\cap F).
$$
Taking $A = X$ gives $\mu^*(E\cup F) = \mu^*(E)+\mu^*(F)$; by induction, $\mu^*$ is finitely additive on pairwise
disjoint members of $\mathcal{M}$.

**(c) Algebra.** $\varnothing,X\in\mathcal{M}$; $\mathcal{M}$ is closed under complements by the symmetry of the
criterion, and under finite unions by (a); finite intersections follow from
$E\cap F = (E^\complement\cup F^\complement)^\complement$. An algebra of sets is exactly a family closed under
these three operations, so $\mathcal{M}$ is an algebra. Adding Step 4 of Theorem 7.20 upgrades it to a
σ-algebra.

**26.** *Strategy: one inequality from a single-cover estimate, the other from compactness.*

**Upper bound.** For every $\varepsilon>0$, $[a,b]\subseteq(a-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon)$, so by definition of the
infimum
$$
\lambda^*([a,b]) \le \lvert(a-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon)\rvert = b-a+2\varepsilon .
$$
Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ gives $\lambda^*([a,b])\le b-a$.

**Lower bound.** Let $\{I_n\}$ be any countable cover of $[a,b]$ by open intervals. By compactness of $[a,b]$
(Heine–Borel), finitely many of them, say $I_1,\dots,I_m$, already cover $[a,b]$. The set
$U = I_1\cup\cdots\cup I_m$ is a finite union of intervals, hence a finite disjoint union of open intervals, and
its total length satisfies
$$
\Bigl\lvert\bigcup_{i=1}^mI_i\Bigr\rvert \le \sum_{i=1}^m\lvert I_i\rvert
$$
(merging two overlapping intervals never increases the total length). Since $[a,b]\subseteq U$,
$b-a\le\lvert U\rvert\le\sum_{i=1}^m\lvert I_i\rvert\le\sum_{n\ge1}\lvert I_n\rvert$. Taking the infimum over
all covers gives $b-a\le\lambda^*([a,b])$.

**Conclusion and the other interval types.** Combining, $\lambda^*([a,b]) = b-a$. For $a<b$ and
$\varepsilon>0$ with $a+\varepsilon<b-\varepsilon$,
$$
[a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon]\subseteq(a,b)\subseteq[a,b]\subseteq(a-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon),
$$
and monotonicity plus the closed case give $b-a-2\varepsilon\le\lambda^*((a,b))\le b-a$, so
$\lambda((a,b)) = b-a$ after letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. The half-open intervals are trapped between
$[a,b]$ and $(a,b)$, so they have the same measure. Finally $\lambda$ and $\lambda^*$ agree on measurable sets,
and intervals are measurable by Theorem 7.25.

**27.** *Strategy: translation is a bijection of the family of open intervals that preserves lengths.*

**Outer measure.** Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ and $t\in\mathbb{R}$. If $A\subseteq\bigcup_nI_n$ then
$A+t\subseteq\bigcup_n(I_n+t)$, and $\lvert I_n+t\rvert = \lvert I_n\rvert$; hence
$\lambda^*(A+t)\le\sum_n\lvert I_n\rvert$ for every cover of $A$, so $\lambda^*(A+t)\le\lambda^*(A)$. Applying the
same argument to $A+t$ and the translation $-t$ gives the reverse inequality, hence
$\lambda^*(A+t) = \lambda^*(A)$.

**Measurability.** Let $E\subseteq\mathbb{R}$. For any test set $A$,
$$
\lambda^*(A\cap(E+t))+\lambda^*(A\setminus(E+t)) = \lambda^*\bigl((A-t)\cap E\bigr)+\lambda^*\bigl((A-t)\setminus E\bigr),
$$
because $(A-t)\cap E = (A\cap(E+t))-t$ and $(A-t)\setminus E = (A\setminus(E+t))-t$, and translation preserves
outer measure. If $E\in\mathcal{L}$ the right-hand side equals $\lambda^*(A-t) = \lambda^*(A)$, so
$E+t\in\mathcal{L}$; applying this to $-t$ gives the converse. Finally $\lambda(E+t) = \lambda^*(E+t) = \lambda^*(E) = \lambda(E)$.

**28.** *Strategy: build a set that meets every coset of $\mathbb{Q}$ exactly once, and let countably many
translates of it cover $[0,1)$ while all of them fit inside an interval of length $3$.*

**The equivalence relation.** Define $x\sim y$ if and only if $x-y\in\mathbb{Q}$. This is reflexive
($0\in\mathbb{Q}$), symmetric ($-\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Q}$) and transitive (a sum of rationals is rational), so
$\sim$ is an equivalence relation, whose classes are the cosets $x+\mathbb{Q}$.

**Choosing representatives.** Every class meets $[0,1)$: given $x\in\mathbb{R}$, pick $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ with
$x\in[n,n+1)$, so $x-n\in[0,1)$ and $x-n\sim x$. By the Axiom of Choice, choose one representative from each
class inside $[0,1)$ and let $V$ be the set of chosen representatives. Then $V\subseteq[0,1)$, and every
$x\in\mathbb{R}$ differs from exactly one element of $V$ by a rational number.

**Disjointness of the translates.** Let $Q = \mathbb{Q}\cap(-1,1) = \{q_1,q_2,\dots\}$ and $V_n = V+q_n$
(ordinary translation). If $v+q_n = v'+q_m$ with $v,v'\in V$, then $v-v' = q_m-q_n\in\mathbb{Q}$, so $v\sim v'$;
distinct classes have distinct representatives, hence $v = v'$ and then $q_n = q_m$, i.e. $n = m$. So the $V_n$
are pairwise disjoint.

**Coverage.** Let $x\in[0,1)$ and let $v\in V$ be the representative of its class. Then $q := x-v$ is rational
and $\lvert q\rvert<1$, so $q\in Q$ and $x = v+q\in V+Q$. Moreover $V+Q\subseteq(-1,2)$ because $V\subseteq[0,1)$
and $\lvert q\rvert<1$. Hence
$$
[0,1)\subseteq V+Q\subseteq(-1,2).
$$

**Contradiction.** Suppose $V$ were Lebesgue measurable. Then every $V_n$ is measurable and
$\lambda(V_n) = \lambda(V)$ by translation invariance (Theorem 7.27), and the $V_n$ are pairwise disjoint. By
countable additivity and monotonicity,
$$
1 = \lambda([0,1)) \le \lambda\Bigl(\bigcup_{n\ge1}V_n\Bigr) = \sum_{n\ge1}\lambda(V_n) = \sum_{n\ge1}\lambda(V) \le \lambda((-1,2)) = 3 .
$$
If $\lambda(V) = 0$ the middle series is $0 < 1$; if $\lambda(V)>0$ it is $+\infty > 3$. Neither is possible, so
$V$ is not Lebesgue measurable.

**29.** *Strategy: approximate the limit from below by an increasing sequence and apply monotone convergence.*

Let $f_n\ge0$ be measurable and put $g_n = \inf_{k\ge n}f_k$.

**Measurability and monotonicity.** Each $g_n$ is measurable because
$\{g_n>a\} = \bigcap_{k\ge n}\{f_k>a\}\in\mathcal{A}$ (Proposition 7.31). Also
$g_1\le g_2\le\cdots$, since the infimum is taken over a shrinking family, and $g_n\ge0$.

**Limit.** For each $x$, the sequence $g_n(x)$ increases and
$$
\sup_ng_n(x) = \sup_n\inf_{k\ge n}f_k(x) = \liminf_{k\to\infty}f_k(x)
$$
by the definition of $\liminf$ in $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$ (the supremum of the increasing sequence of infima).

**Fatou.** Since $g_n\le f_n$ pointwise, monotonicity of the integral gives $\int g_n\le\int f_n$, hence
$\lim_n\int g_n\le\liminf_n\int f_n$ (a limit is at most a liminf). Monotone convergence (Theorem 7.43) applied to
$(g_n)$ yields
$$
\int\liminf_nf_n\,d\mu = \int\sup_ng_n\,d\mu = \lim_n\int g_n\,d\mu \le \liminf_n\int f_n\,d\mu . \qquad\blacksquare
$$

**30.** *Strategy: apply Fatou to the non-negative functions $2g-\lvert f_n-f\rvert$, which is legitimate precisely
because $2g$ is integrable.*

Let $N$ be a null set outside which $f_n\to f$ and $\lvert f_n\rvert\le g$ for all $n$; replacing $f_n,f$ by $0$ on
$N$ changes no integral (Proposition 7.41.3), so assume the hypotheses hold everywhere. Then
$\lvert f\rvert\le g$, so $f$ is integrable, and $\lvert f_n-f\rvert\le2g$ with $\int2g\,d\mu<\infty$ — this is
the **first** use of the finiteness of $\int g$.

The functions $h_n = 2g-\lvert f_n-f\rvert$ are measurable and non-negative. By Fatou (Theorem 7.47),
$$
\int\liminf_n h_n\,d\mu \le \liminf_n\int h_n\,d\mu .
$$
Now $\liminf_nh_n = 2g-\limsup_n\lvert f_n-f\rvert = 2g$ because $\lvert f_n-f\rvert\to0$; and by linearity
(Theorem 7.46) $\int h_n = \int2g-\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert$. Hence
$$
\int2g\,d\mu \le \int2g\,d\mu - \limsup_n\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert\,d\mu .
$$
Since $\int2g<\infty$ — the **second** use of finiteness — we may subtract it, obtaining
$\limsup_n\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert\le0$, so $\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert\to0$. Finally
$$
\Bigl\lvert\int f_n-\int f\Bigr\rvert = \Bigl\lvert\int(f_n-f)\Bigr\rvert \le \int\lvert f_n-f\rvert \to 0
$$
by Theorem 7.46.

**Bounded convergence.** If $\mu(X)<\infty$ and $\lvert f_n\rvert\le M$ with $f_n\to f$ a.e., take $g = M$; then
$\int g\,d\mu = M\mu(X)<\infty$, so $g$ is integrable and Theorem 7.50 applies.

**31.** *Strategy: concavity of the logarithm for Young, then normalisation and integration for Hölder.*

**Young.** Let $1<p<\infty$ and $q = p/(p-1)$, so $\frac1p+\frac1q = 1$. If $a = 0$ or $b = 0$ the inequality
$0\le a^p/p+b^q/q$ is clear. Otherwise $a^p,b^q>0$ and concavity of $\log$ gives, with weights $1/p$ and $1/q$
(which are non-negative and sum to $1$),
$$
\log(ab) = \frac1p\log(a^p)+\frac1q\log(b^q) \le \log\Bigl(\frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}\Bigr).
$$
Exponentiating (the exponential is increasing) gives $ab\le\frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}$. Equality in the
concavity step occurs exactly when $a^p = b^q$.

**Hölder.** Let $f,g$ be measurable and $q$ the conjugate exponent of $p$.

*Case $\lVert f\rVert_p = 0$.* Then $\lvert f\rvert^p = 0$ a.e., so $f = 0$ a.e. and $fg = 0$ a.e.; both sides of
Hölder are $0$.

*Case $\lVert f\rVert_p = \infty$ or $\lVert g\rVert_q = \infty$.* The right-hand side is $+\infty$ and there is
nothing to prove.

*Case $0<\lVert f\rVert_p,\lVert g\rVert_q<\infty$.* Put $F = f/\lVert f\rVert_p$ and $G = g/\lVert g\rVert_q$, so
$\lVert F\rVert_p = \lVert G\rVert_q = 1$. Young's inequality applied pointwise to
$a = \lvert F(x)\rvert$, $b = \lvert G(x)\rvert$ reads
$$
\lvert F(x)G(x)\rvert \le \frac{\lvert F(x)\rvert^p}{p}+\frac{\lvert G(x)\rvert^q}{q},
$$
an inequality that holds at every point (trivially, as $+\infty\le+\infty$, where either side is infinite).
Integrating and using Corollary 7.44,
$$
\int\lvert FG\rvert\,d\mu \le \frac1p\lVert F\rVert_p^p+\frac1q\lVert G\rVert_q^q = \frac1p+\frac1q = 1 .
$$
Multiplying by $\lVert f\rVert_p\lVert g\rVert_q$ gives $\int\lvert fg\rvert\le\lVert f\rVert_p\lVert g\rVert_q$.
The cases $p = 1$ ($q = \infty$) and $p = \infty$ are immediate from
$\lvert fg\rvert\le\lVert g\rVert_\infty\lvert f\rvert$ a.e.

**32.** *Strategy: sandwich $f$ between step functions whose integrals converge to the Riemann integral, and use
completeness of $\lambda$ to conclude measurability.*

Let $\lvert f\rvert\le M$ on $[a,b]$ and $I = \int_a^b f$ (Riemann). By the Riemann criterion, choose partitions
$P_n = \{x_0^{(n)}<\cdots<x_{m_n}^{(n)}\}$ with $U(f,P_n)-L(f,P_n)<1/n$. Define step functions
$$
u_n = \sum_{i=1}^{m_n}M_i^{(n)}\mathbf{1}_{[x_{i-1}^{(n)},x_i^{(n)})},
\qquad
\ell_n = \sum_{i=1}^{m_n}m_i^{(n)}\mathbf{1}_{[x_{i-1}^{(n)},x_i^{(n)})},
$$
with $M_i^{(n)} = \sup_{I_i^{(n)}}f$ and $m_i^{(n)} = \inf_{I_i^{(n)}}f$. Each is Borel measurable and bounded,
with $\ell_n\le f\le u_n$ on $[a,b]$ and
$$
\int_{[a,b]}u_n\,d\lambda = U(f,P_n), \qquad \int_{[a,b]}\ell_n\,d\lambda = L(f,P_n)
$$
by Theorem 7.26 and finite additivity of $\lambda$.

Set $u = \inf_nu_n$ and $\ell = \sup_n\ell_n$; both are measurable (Proposition 7.33) and $\ell\le f\le u$. Since
$u\le u_n$ for every $n$,
$$
\int u\,d\lambda \le \inf_n\int u_n\,d\lambda = \lim_nU(f,P_n) = I,
$$
the last equality because $L(f,P_n)\le I\le U(f,P_n)$ and $U(f,P_n)-L(f,P_n)\to0$. Symmetrically
$\int\ell\,d\lambda\ge I$. As $\ell\le u$ and
$\int(u-\ell)\le I-I = 0$ with $u-\ell\ge0$, Proposition 7.41.4 gives $u = \ell$ a.e., hence $f = u$ a.e.

Since Lebesgue measure is complete and $u$ is measurable, $f$ is Lebesgue measurable; and by
Proposition 7.41.3,
$$
\int_{[a,b]}f\,d\lambda = \int_{[a,b]}u\,d\lambda \le I \le \int_{[a,b]}\ell\,d\lambda = \int_{[a,b]}u\,d\lambda,
$$
so all these numbers coincide and equal $I$.

**33.** *Strategy: discard the first $N-1$ terms so that the first remaining set has finite measure, then use
Theorem 7.14.*

**The general form.** Let $A_1\supseteq A_2\supseteq\cdots$, $A = \bigcap_nA_n$, and suppose $\mu(A_N)<\infty$ for
some $N$. Define $B_k = A_{N+k-1}$ for $k\ge1$; then $B_1 = A_N$ has finite measure, $B_k\downarrow A$, and
$\lim_k\mu(B_k) = \lim_n\mu(A_n)$ because the two sequences differ in finitely many terms. Applying Theorem 7.14
to $(B_k)$ gives $\mu(A) = \lim_k\mu(B_k) = \lim_n\mu(A_n)$.

**A counterexample with every measure infinite.** On $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L},\lambda)$ let $A_n = [n,\infty)$.
Then $A_n\downarrow\varnothing$, $\lambda(A_n) = \infty$ for every $n$ and
$0 = \lambda(\varnothing)\ne\infty = \lim_n\lambda(A_n)$.

**Why the hypothesis is the borderline.** The proof writes $C_n = A_N\setminus A_n$ and uses
$\mu(A_N\setminus A_n) = \mu(A_N)-\mu(A_n)$; this requires $\mu(A_N)<\infty$. If $\mu(A_N) = \infty$ then all the
relevant quantities are $+\infty$ and the subtraction is undefined. The example shows that the hypothesis cannot
simply be dropped; what survives in general is only the inequality
$\mu\bigl(\bigcap_nA_n\bigr)\le\lim_n\mu(A_n)$.

**34.** *Strategy: bound the measure of the limsup by the tail of the series.*

**Borel–Cantelli.** For each $n$,
$$
\mu\Bigl(\limsup_kA_k\Bigr) = \mu\Bigl(\bigcap_{n}\bigcup_{k\ge n}A_k\Bigr) \le \mu\Bigl(\bigcup_{k\ge n}A_k\Bigr) \le \sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\mu(A_k),
$$
using monotonicity and countable subadditivity. The tail of a convergent series of non-negative terms tends to
$0$, so a fixed non-negative number bounded by every tail must be $0$.

**Almost-sure convergence.** Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $A_n(\varepsilon) = \{\lvert f_n-f\rvert>\varepsilon\}$.
By hypothesis $\sum_n\mu(A_n(\varepsilon))<\infty$, so $\mu\bigl(\limsup_nA_n(\varepsilon)\bigr) = 0$: almost
every $x$ belongs to only finitely many of the sets $A_n(\varepsilon)$, i.e. for almost every $x$ there is
$N(x,\varepsilon)$ with $\lvert f_n(x)-f(x)\rvert\le\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N(x,\varepsilon)$.

Now let $\varepsilon = 1/m$ and let $N_m$ be the corresponding exceptional null set. Then
$N = \bigcup_mN_m$ is a countable union of null sets, hence null, and for $x\notin N$ and every $m$ there is
$N(x,m)$ with $\lvert f_n(x)-f(x)\rvert\le1/m$ for all $n\ge N(x,m)$. Given $\eta>0$, choose $m>1/\eta$; then
$\lvert f_n(x)-f(x)\rvert<\eta$ eventually. Hence $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for every $x\notin N$, that is, $f_n\to f$ a.e.

**35.** *Strategy: (a) and (b) are monotone convergence applied to partial sums; (c) is a matter of direction.*

**(a)** $\nu(\varnothing) = \int\varnothing = 0$. If $A = \bigsqcup_nA_n$, then
$s_N = \sum_{n\le N}f\mathbf{1}_{A_n}\uparrow f\mathbf{1}_A$, so by Theorem 7.43 and finite additivity,
$$
\nu(A) = \lim_N\int s_N\,d\mu = \lim_N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nu(A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_n).
$$
If $\mu(A) = 0$ then $f\mathbf{1}_A = 0$ a.e., so $\nu(A) = 0$: $\nu\ll\mu$.

**(b)** For $A\in\mathcal{A}$, writing $g\mathbf{1}_A$ as a limit of increasing simple functions and using (a),
$$
\rho(A) = \int_A g\,d\nu = \int g\mathbf{1}_A\,d\nu = \int fg\mathbf{1}_A\,d\mu = \int_A fg\,d\mu,
$$
where the third equality follows by the standard approximation argument: it holds for indicators (by the
definition of $\nu$), hence for simple functions by linearity, hence for all measurable $g\ge0$ by monotone
convergence.

**(c)** On $(\mathbb{R},\lambda)$ take $f = \mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}$. Then $\nu(\mathbb{R}) = \lambda((0,1)) = 1<\infty$
while $\mu(\mathbb{R}) = \infty$. Parts (a) and (b) show that *every* non-negative measurable $f$ produces a
measure $\nu\ll\mu$ with a density, and they show how to manipulate such measures. They do not show that every
$\nu\ll\mu$ arises this way: that is the content of the Radon–Nikodym theorem (7.71), whose proof is
substantially harder and which requires σ-finiteness. The present problem is the easy direction of the
correspondence "densities $\leftrightarrow$ absolutely continuous measures".

**36.** *Strategy: an upper bound from Chebyshev and a lower bound from the definition of the essential
supremum.*

Assume $\lVert f\rVert_\infty>0$ (if $f = 0$ a.e. all norms vanish and the result is trivial) and let
$M = \lVert f\rVert_\infty$.

**Upper bound.** For $p<\infty$, $\lvert f\rvert\le M$ a.e., so
$\lVert f\rVert_p\le M\mu(X)^{1/p}$. Since $\mu(X)<\infty$, $\mu(X)^{1/p}\to1$ as $p\to\infty$, hence
$$
\limsup_{p\to\infty}\lVert f\rVert_p\le M .
$$

**Lower bound.** Let $0<\alpha<M$. By definition of the essential supremum, the set
$E_\alpha = \{\lvert f\rvert>\alpha\}$ is not null (otherwise $\alpha$ would be an essential upper bound smaller
than $M$). Then
$$
\lVert f\rVert_p \ge \Bigl(\int_{E_\alpha}\lvert f\rvert^p\,d\mu\Bigr)^{1/p} \ge \alpha\,\mu(E_\alpha)^{1/p},
$$
and $\mu(E_\alpha)^{1/p}\to1$ as $p\to\infty$ because $\mu(E_\alpha)\in(0,\infty]$ — and
$\mu(E_\alpha)\le\mu(X)<\infty$ here, so the limit is $1$. Hence
$\liminf_p\lVert f\rVert_p\ge\alpha$ for every $\alpha<M$, so $\liminf_p\lVert f\rVert_p\ge M$.

Combining the two bounds, $\lVert f\rVert_p\to M = \lVert f\rVert_\infty$. Note where the hypothesis $\mu(X)<\infty$ enters: it makes $\mu(E_\alpha)$ finite, so that $\mu(E_\alpha)^{1/p}\to1$ rather than $\to\infty$.

**37.** *Strategy: compute the inner integral with the fundamental theorem, then observe that the two orders
differ; finally show $\lvert f\rvert$ is not integrable by Tonelli.*

**(a)** For fixed $y\in(0,1)$,
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigl(\frac{-x}{(x+y)^2}\Bigr) = \frac{-(x+y)^2+2x(x+y)}{(x+y)^4} = \frac{x-y}{(x+y)^3} .
$$
Hence
$$
\int_0^1\frac{x-y}{(x+y)^3}\,dx = \Bigl[\frac{-x}{(x+y)^2}\Bigr]_{x=0}^{x=1} = -\frac{1}{(1+y)^2},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1f\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1-\frac{dy}{(1+y)^2} = \Bigl[\frac{1}{1+y}\Bigr]_0^1 = \frac12-1 = -\frac12 .
$$
Since $f(y,x) = -f(x,y)$, the other order gives $+\frac12$. The two iterated integrals differ.

**(b)** For fixed $y\in(0,1)$ substitute $x = yt$, $dx = y\,dt$, for $t\in(0,1/y)$:
$$
\int_0^1\frac{\lvert x-y\rvert}{(x+y)^3}\,dx = \frac1y\int_0^{1/y}\frac{\lvert t-1\rvert}{(t+1)^3}\,dt \ge \frac{c}{y},
$$
where $c := \int_0^{1}\frac{\lvert t-1\rvert}{(t+1)^3}\,dt>0$ (the integrand is positive on $(0,1)$). Therefore
by Tonelli (the integrand is non-negative)
$$
\int_{(0,1)^2}\lvert f\rvert\,d(\lambda\times\lambda) = \int_0^1\Bigl(\int_0^1\lvert f(x,y)\rvert\,dx\Bigr)dy \ge c\int_0^1\frac{dy}{y} = \infty .
$$

**(c)** Fubini's theorem (7.66) requires $f\in L^1((0,1)^2)$, which by (b) fails. The hypothesis that fails is
exactly $\int\lvert f\rvert\,d(\mu\times\nu)<\infty$; the mere existence of both iterated integrals says nothing.
Tonelli's theorem cannot be invoked either, since $\lvert f\rvert$ is not non-negative-valued in the required way
— Tonelli applies to $\lvert f\rvert$ and yields $+\infty$, which is consistent but gives no equality of the
iterated integrals of $f$.

**38.** *Strategy: every element of $\bar{\mathcal{A}}$ is a measurable set plus a negligible set; show that the
negligible part can always be absorbed, then verify well-definedness.*

**(a) $\bar{\mathcal{A}}$ is a σ-algebra.** Clearly $X = X\cup\varnothing\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}$. For complements, let
$E = A\cup N$ with $A\in\mathcal{A}$ and $N\subseteq M$, where $M\in\mathcal{A}$ and $\mu(M) = 0$. Then
$$
E^\complement = A^\complement\cap N^\complement = \underbrace{(A^\complement\cap M^\complement)}_{\in\,\mathcal{A}}\ \cup\ \underbrace{(A^\complement\cap M\setminus N)}_{\subseteq\,M,\ \text{null}} ,
$$
a union of a measurable set and a subset of the null set $M$; hence $E^\complement\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}$. For
countable unions, if $E_n = A_n\cup N_n$ with $N_n\subseteq M_n$ and $\mu(M_n) = 0$, then
$$
\bigcup_nE_n = \Bigl(\bigcup_nA_n\Bigr)\cup\Bigl(\bigcup_nN_n\Bigr), \qquad \bigcup_nN_n\subseteq\bigcup_nM_n,
$$
and $\mu\bigl(\bigcup_nM_n\bigr) = 0$ by subadditivity, so $\bigcup_nE_n\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}$. Finally
$\mathcal{A}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}$ (take $N = \varnothing$) and $\mathcal{N}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}$
(take $A = \varnothing$).

**(b) Well-definedness.** Suppose $A_1\cup N_1 = A_2\cup N_2$ with $N_i\subseteq M_i$ and $\mu(M_i) = 0$. Then
$A_1\setminus A_2\subseteq N_2\subseteq M_2$, so $\mu(A_1\setminus A_2) = 0$, and symmetrically
$\mu(A_2\setminus A_1) = 0$. The two disjoint decompositions
$$
A_1 = (A_1\cap A_2)\sqcup(A_1\setminus A_2), \qquad A_2 = (A_1\cap A_2)\sqcup(A_2\setminus A_1)
$$
give, by finite additivity in $[0,\infty]$,
$$
\mu(A_1) = \mu(A_1\cap A_2)+0 = \mu(A_1\cap A_2), \qquad \mu(A_2) = \mu(A_1\cap A_2)+0 = \mu(A_1\cap A_2),
$$
hence $\mu(A_1) = \mu(A_2)$: the value $\bar\mu(A\cup N)$ does not depend on the representation.

**(c) $\bar\mu$ is a complete measure extending $\mu$.** $\bar\mu(\varnothing) = \mu(\varnothing) = 0$. Let
$E_n = A_n\cup N_n$ be pairwise disjoint, with $N_n\subseteq M_n$ null, and put $E = \bigcup_nE_n$. Then
$E = A\cup N$ with $A = \bigcup_nA_n$ and $N = \bigcup_nN_n$ null, and the $A_n$ are pairwise disjoint because the
$E_n$ are. Countable additivity of $\mu$ gives
$$
\bar\mu(E) = \mu\Bigl(\bigcup_nA_n\Bigr) = \sum_{n\ge1}\mu(A_n) = \sum_{n\ge1}\bar\mu(E_n).
$$
The extension property is immediate: $\bar\mu(A\cup\varnothing) = \mu(A)$ for $A\in\mathcal{A}$. For completeness,
suppose $\bar\mu(E) = 0$ with $E = A\cup N$, so $\mu(A) = 0$. Then $E\subseteq A\cup M$, a measurable set of
$\mu$-measure $0$, so every subset $F\subseteq E$ is a null set, i.e. $F\in\mathcal{N}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}$
with $\bar\mu(F) = 0$.
