## Beginner

**1.** **Strategy.** Each degree is computed as a product of degrees of simple steps; at each step we must prove that the new element is *not* in the field built so far, which is an irreducibility statement about its minimal polynomial.

(a) $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$, since $x^2-3$ has no rational root. Next, $x^2-5$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3)$: if $\sqrt5 = a+b\sqrt3$ with $a,b \in \mathbb{Q}$, then $5 = a^2+3b^2+2ab\sqrt3$; since $1,\sqrt3$ are $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent, $ab = 0$. If $a = 0$ then $5 = 3b^2$, i.e. $5/3 = b^2$ is a rational square, impossible since $5/3$ is not a square of a rational in lowest terms. If $b = 0$ then $5 = a^2$, impossible. So the degree is $2$, and $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3,\sqrt5):\mathbb{Q}] = 2\cdot 2 = 4$ by the tower law.

(b) $x^3-3$ is Eisenstein at $3$ ($3 \mid 3$, $9 \nmid 3$), hence irreducible, so the degree is $3$.

(c) $x^4-2$ is Eisenstein at $2$, hence irreducible, so the degree is $4$.

(d) $\zeta_6 = e^{2\pi i/6} = e^{\pi i/3} = \frac12 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i$, so $\zeta_6 = \frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}$ and $\zeta_6^2 - \zeta_6 + 1 = 0$. The polynomial $x^2-x+1$ has discriminant $-3$, not a square in $\mathbb{Q}$, so it is irreducible; the degree is $2$ (and $\varphi(6) = 2$, consistent with §7.12).

(e) $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. Since $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and $x^2+1$ has no real root, $x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, so the second step has degree $2$ and $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,i):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$.

**2.** **Strategy.** Exhibit a polynomial vanishing at the element, then prove it is the minimal one by showing it is irreducible (or by computing the degree of the field generated by the element).

(a) $\sqrt5$ satisfies $x^2-5$, which has no rational root, so $x^2-5$ is irreducible and is the minimal polynomial.

(b) If $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{3}+1$ then $(\alpha-1)^3 = 3$, so $\alpha$ satisfies $(x-1)^3-3 = x^3-3x^2+3x-4$. Substituting $x \mapsto x+1$ is an automorphism of $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and recovers $x^3-3$, which is Eisenstein at $3$; hence $x^3-3x^2+3x-4$ is irreducible. Minimal polynomial: $x^3-3x^2+3x-4$ (degree $3$).

(c) For $\alpha = \sqrt2+i$:
$$\alpha^2 = 1+2i\sqrt2 \;\Longrightarrow\; (\alpha^2-1)^2 = -8 \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha^4-2\alpha^2+9 = 0.$$
To see that $x^4-2x^2+9$ is minimal, note $\alpha\bar\alpha = 3$, so $\bar\alpha = 3/\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ and $\sqrt2 = \frac{\alpha+\bar\alpha}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, $i = \alpha-\sqrt2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$; hence $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,i)$ has degree $4$, so the minimal polynomial has degree $4$ and is $x^4-2x^2+9$.

(d) Let $\zeta = \zeta_5$ and $\eta = \zeta+\zeta^{-1}$. Then $\eta^2 = \zeta^2+2+\zeta^{-2}$, and from $1+\zeta+\zeta^2+\zeta^3+\zeta^4 = 0$ we get $\zeta^2+\zeta^{-2} = -1-\eta$. Hence $\eta^2 = 2-1-\eta = 1-\eta$, that is, $\eta^2+\eta-1 = 0$. Since $\eta = 2\cos\frac{2\pi}{5} = \frac{\sqrt5-1}{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$, the polynomial $x^2+x-1$ is irreducible and minimal.

**3.** **Strategy.** A splitting field is generated by the roots; identify the roots explicitly and the field they generate.

(a) $x^4-1 = (x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)$; the roots are $\pm1,\pm i$, so the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, of degree $2$.

(b) $x^3-1 = (x-1)(x^2+x+1)$; the roots are $1,\omega,\omega^2$ with $\omega = \frac{-1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}$, so the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\omega) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, of degree $2$.

(c) Over $\mathbb{R}$, $x^2+1 = (x-i)(x+i)$; the splitting field is $\mathbb{C}$, of degree $2$ over $\mathbb{R}$.

(d) Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, $x^2+x+1$ has no root ($0 \mapsto 1$, $1 \mapsto 1$), so it is irreducible and $F := \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2+x+1)$ is a field with $4$ elements. If $\alpha$ is the class of $x$ then $\alpha^2 = \alpha+1$, and the roots of $x^2+x+1$ are $\alpha$ and $\alpha+1$ (their sum is $-1 = 1$); both lie in $F$, so $F$ is the splitting field and its degree is $2$.

**4.** **Strategy.** An automorphism of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ must send $\sqrt5$ to a root of $x^2-5$ inside the field; there are two roots, giving two automorphisms. For $\sqrt[3]{3}$ there is only one root inside the (real) field.

For $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$: define $\sigma(a+b\sqrt5) = a-b\sqrt5$ for $a,b \in \mathbb{Q}$. This is a ring homomorphism because it is induced by the substitution $\sqrt5 \mapsto -\sqrt5$, which preserves the relation $(\sqrt5)^2 = 5$; it is bijective (it is its own inverse) and fixes $\mathbb{Q}$. Together with the identity, these are all the automorphisms, because $\sigma(\sqrt5)$ must satisfy $\sigma(\sqrt5)^2 = 5$, so $\sigma(\sqrt5) = \pm\sqrt5$. The fixed field is $\{a+b\sqrt5 : b = 0\} = \mathbb{Q}$, so $E/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois with group $C_2$.

For $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{3}) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$: any automorphism sends $\sqrt[3]3$ to a root in $K$ of $x^3-3$. The roots are $\sqrt[3]3, \omega\sqrt[3]3, \omega^2\sqrt[3]3$; the last two are non-real, so the only root in $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is $\sqrt[3]3$ itself. Hence $\sigma = \operatorname{id}$ is the only automorphism, $K^{\operatorname{Aut}} = K \ne \mathbb{Q}$, and $K/\mathbb{Q}$ is not Galois. (It is also not normal: $x^3-3$ has one root in $K$ but does not split there.)

**5.** **Strategy.** Compute the degree first, then exhibit that many automorphisms; the group structure follows from the orders.

$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3,\sqrt5):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ by Exercise 1(a), so $E$ has $\mathbb{Q}$-basis $1,\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\sqrt{15}$ and every element is uniquely $a+b\sqrt3+c\sqrt5+d\sqrt{15}$. An automorphism is determined by $\sqrt3 \mapsto \pm\sqrt3$ and $\sqrt5 \mapsto \pm\sqrt5$, and each of the four sign choices defines a well-defined automorphism (it permutes the basis and preserves the relations $\sqrt3^2 = 3$, $\sqrt5^2 = 5$). Explicitly:
$$\operatorname{id}, \quad \sigma : \sqrt3 \mapsto -\sqrt3,\ \sqrt5\mapsto\sqrt5, \quad \tau : \sqrt3 \mapsto \sqrt3,\ \sqrt5 \mapsto -\sqrt5, \quad \sigma\tau : \sqrt3 \mapsto -\sqrt3,\ \sqrt5 \mapsto -\sqrt5.$$
Every non-identity element has order $2$, so $G \cong V_4$ (a group of order $4$ with no element of order $4$ is Klein four).

Subgroups and fixed fields:
| Subgroup | Fixed field |
|---|---|
| $V_4$ | $\mathbb{Q}$ |
| $\langle\sigma\rangle$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ (since $\sigma$ fixes $\sqrt5$ and not $\sqrt3$) |
| $\langle\tau\rangle$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3)$ |
| $\langle\sigma\tau\rangle$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{15})$ (since $\sigma\tau(\sqrt{15}) = (-\sqrt3)(-\sqrt5) = \sqrt{15}$) |
| $\{1\}$ | $E$ |

Each fixed field has the degree $[G:H]$ predicted by Theorem 7.9.1(b): $1,2,2,2,4$.

**6.** **Strategy.** $f$ is separable iff $\gcd(f,f') = 1$ (Lemma 5.5.2); compute the formal derivative in the stated characteristic.

(a) $f' = 2x$, $\gcd(x^2+1,2x) = 1$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: separable (roots $\pm i$ are distinct).

(b) In characteristic $2$, $f' = 2x = 0$, so $\gcd(f,f') = \gcd(x^2+1,0) = x^2+1 \ne 1$: not separable. Indeed $x^2+1 = (x+1)^2$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$.

(c) $f' = 3x^2-1 = -1 \ne 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_3$, so $\gcd(f,f') = 1$: separable. Indeed $x^3-x = x(x-1)(x+1)$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$.

(d) $f' = 2x+1 = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$, so $\gcd = 1$: separable. Its roots lie in $\mathbb{F}_4$ and are distinct.

(e) $f' = px^{p-1} = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p(t)[x]$, so $\gcd(f,f') = f \ne 1$: inseparable. Indeed $x^p-t = (x-t^{1/p})^p$ in the extension $\mathbb{F}_p(t^{1/p})$.

(f) $f' = 4x^3$ and $x^4+1$ has nonzero constant term, so $x \nmid f$ and $\gcd(f,4x^3) = 1$: separable. (Also Theorem 5.5.4: characteristic $0$.)
**7.** **Strategy.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$ use Eisenstein after a translation; over $\mathbb{F}_2$ use the Frobenius $(x+1)^2 = x^2+1$; over $\mathbb{F}_3$ try quadratic factors.

Over $\mathbb{Q}$: $\Phi_8 = x^4+1$. Consider $(x+1)^4+1 = x^4+4x^3+6x^2+4x+2$; all non-leading coefficients but the last are divisible by $2$, and the constant term $2$ is not divisible by $4$, so this polynomial is Eisenstein at $2$ and irreducible. Substituting $x \mapsto x-1$ keeps irreducibility, so $x^4+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$; the factorisation is $x^4+1$ itself.

Over $\mathbb{F}_2$: $(x+1)^4 = x^4 + 4x^3+6x^2+4x+1 = x^4+1$ because every coefficient $4,6,4$ is even; so $x^4+1 = (x+1)^4$ in $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$. This is the factorisation showing inseparability: the only root is $x = 1$, with multiplicity $4$.

Over $\mathbb{F}_3$: try $(x^2+ax+b)(x^2-ax+d)$ with $bd = 1$ (so $b = d = 1$ or $b = d = -1$) and $b+d-a^2 = 0$ (coefficient of $x^2$), $a(d-b) = 0$ (coefficient of $x$). With $b = d = 1$ we need $2 = a^2$, and $2$ is not a square modulo $3$. With $b = d = -1$ we need $-2 = a^2$, i.e. $1 = a^2$, satisfied by $a = \pm1$. Hence
$$x^4+1 = (x^2+x-1)(x^2-x-1) \quad \text{over } \mathbb{F}_3,$$
and neither quadratic has a root in $\mathbb{F}_3$ (values of $x^2+x-1$ at $0,1,2$ are $2,1,2$), so both are irreducible. The derivative check $\gcd(x^4+1,4x^3) = 1$ shows this factorisation is separable.

**8.** **Strategy.** Use Proposition 7.10.4; then apply Theorem 7.10.3, remembering its hypothesis that the polynomial be separable.

(a) $x^2-7$: $\Delta = 0-4(-7) = 28$, not a square.

(b) $x^2+x+1$: $\Delta = 1-4 = -3$, not a square (it is negative).

(c) $x^3-3x+1$: $p = -3$, $q = 1$, so $\Delta = -4(-27)-27(1) = 108-27 = 81 = 9^2$, a square.

(d) $x^3-1$: $p = 0$, $q = -1$, so $\Delta = -27\cdot 1 = -27$, not a square. (Direct check: the roots are $1,\omega,\omega^2$ and $\Delta = (1-\omega)^2(1-\omega^2)^2(\omega-\omega^2)^2 = 9\cdot(-3) = -27$.)

(e) $x^3-7x+7$: $\Delta = -4(-343)-27\cdot49 = 1372-1323 = 49 = 7^2$, a square.

(f) $x^4-2$: $p = q = 0$, $r = -2$, so $\Delta = 256r^3 = 256\cdot(-8) = -2048$, not a square.

Squares in $\mathbb{Q}$: only (c) and (e). By Theorem 7.10.3, the Galois group is contained in $A_n$ exactly for (c) and (e). Since both are irreducible of degree $3$, their groups are transitive subgroups of $S_3$ contained in $A_3$, hence equal to $A_3$. For (a) and (b) the group is $C_2$ (not inside $A_2$). For (d) the polynomial is reducible and its splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ with group $C_2$ acting as a transposition of $\omega,\omega^2$; a transposition is odd, consistent with $\Delta = -27$ not being a square. For (f) the group is not contained in $A_4$ (it is $D_4$, Example 9.6).

**9.** **Strategy.** For a quadratic $x^2+bx+c$ the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt\Delta)$ with $\Delta = b^2-4c$ (Example 9.2).

(a) $x^2-3$: $\Delta = 12$, roots $\pm\sqrt3$, splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3)$, group $C_2$.

(b) $x^2+3$: $\Delta = -12$, roots $\pm i\sqrt3$, splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(i\sqrt3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, group $C_2$.

(c) $x^2-4x+1$: $\Delta = 16-4 = 12$, roots $2\pm\sqrt3$, splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3)$, group $C_2$.

(d) $x^2-4x+4 = (x-2)^2$: the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}$ and the group is trivial.

**10.** **Strategy.** Rational root test, then the discriminant criterion.

*Irreducibility.* The only rational candidates are $\pm1,\pm7$. We compute $f(1) = 1-7+7 = 1$, $f(-1) = -1+7+7 = 13$, $f(7) = 343-49+7 = 301$, $f(-7) = -343+49+7 = -287$. None vanishes, so there is no linear factor and the cubic is irreducible.

*Discriminant.* $p = -7$, $q = 7$, so
$$\Delta = -4p^3-27q^2 = -4(-343)-27\cdot 49 = 1372-1323 = 49 = 7^2,$$
a square in $\mathbb{Q}$.

*Group.* By Theorem 7.10.3, $G \subseteq A_3$. Since $f$ is irreducible, $G$ is transitive of degree $3$ (Lemma 5.8.2), so $3 \mid |G|$; the only transitive subgroups of $S_3$ are $A_3$ (order $3$) and $S_3$ (order $6$), and $G \subseteq A_3$ forces $G = A_3 \cong C_3$. Hence $[E:\mathbb{Q}] = |G| = 3$ by Corollary 7.7.2: the splitting field has degree $3$, which also shows that adjoining one root already gives all three.

**11.** **Strategy.** $\varphi$ is multiplicative on coprime arguments, with $\varphi(p^k) = p^{k-1}(p-1)$; then apply Gauss–Wantzel: the regular $n$-gon is constructible iff $\varphi(n)$ is a power of $2$ (Theorem 7.13.6).

| $n$ | $\varphi(n)$ | power of $2$? | $n$-gon constructible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $7$ | $6$ | no | no |
| $9$ | $6$ | no | no |
| $12$ | $\varphi(4)\varphi(3) = 2\cdot2 = 4$ | yes | yes |
| $15$ | $\varphi(3)\varphi(5) = 2\cdot4 = 8$ | yes | yes |
| $16$ | $8$ | yes | yes |
| $17$ | $16$ | yes | yes (Gauss) |
| $20$ | $\varphi(4)\varphi(5) = 2\cdot4 = 8$ | yes | yes |
| $24$ | $\varphi(8)\varphi(3) = 4\cdot2 = 8$ | yes | yes |
| $25$ | $20$ | no | no |
| $30$ | $\varphi(2)\varphi(3)\varphi(5) = 1\cdot2\cdot4 = 8$ | yes | yes |

The arithmetic form of Gauss–Wantzel agrees: $12 = 2^2\cdot3$, $15 = 3\cdot5$, $16 = 2^4$, $17$ Fermat prime, $20 = 2^2\cdot5$, $24 = 2^3\cdot3$, $30 = 2\cdot3\cdot5$ are products of a power of $2$ and distinct Fermat primes ($3,5,17$), while $7$ (Fermat prime and $\varphi = 6$), $9 = 3^2$ (repeated prime), $25 = 5^2$ fail.

**12.** **Strategy.** An extension is normal iff every irreducible polynomial with a root in it splits there (Definition 5.6.1); for splitting fields use Theorem 7.5.1.

* $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$: normal. It is the splitting field of $x^5-1$: the roots are $1,\zeta_5,\dots,\zeta_5^4$, all powers of $\zeta_5$.
* $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$: not normal. $x^3-2$ has the root $\sqrt[3]2$ in the field but the other two roots are non-real, and the field is real.
* $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt[3]{2})$: not normal, for the same reason: it is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$, so $x^3-2$ cannot split there.
* $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$: not normal: $x^4-2$ has the root $\sqrt[4]2$ in the field, but $\pm i\sqrt[4]2$ are non-real.
* $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$: normal — it is the splitting field of $(x^2-2)(x^2-3)$.
* $\mathbb{Q}(\omega,\sqrt[3]{2})$: normal — it is the splitting field of $x^3-2$, since all three roots $\sqrt[3]2, \omega\sqrt[3]2, \omega^2\sqrt[3]2$ lie in it.

## Intermediate

**13.** **Strategy.** For degree $2$ the minimal polynomial has degree $2$ and its second root is visible from the coefficients; separability is exactly where characteristic $2$ can fail.

Suppose $[E:F] = 2$ and $\operatorname{char} F \ne 2$. Take $\alpha \in E\setminus F$; then $[F(\alpha):F] \ge 2$ and divides $2$, so $E = F(\alpha)$ and $m_\alpha = x^2+bx+c$ is irreducible. Its discriminant $\Delta = b^2-4c$ is nonzero (a repeated root would make $x^2+bx+c$ a square $(x-r)^2$, which is reducible) and the roots are
$$\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4c}}{2},$$
which lie in $E$ because $2 \ne 0$ in $F$ and $\sqrt{b^2-4c} = 2\alpha+b \in E$. So $E$ is the splitting field of the separable polynomial $x^2+bx+c$, hence Galois by Theorem 7.7.1, and $|\operatorname{Gal}(E/F)| = [E:F] = 2$, so the group is $C_2$.

In characteristic $2$ let $F = \mathbb{F}_2(t)$ and $E = F(\alpha)$ with $\alpha^2 = t$. Then $x^2-t = (x-\alpha)^2$ in $E[x]$, so $E$ *is* the splitting field and the extension is **normal**; but $m_\alpha = x^2-t$ has derivative $2x = 0$, so it is inseparable, $[E:F]_s = 1 < 2 = [E:F]$. Hence $E/F$ is not Galois. Concretely, any $F$-automorphism sends $\alpha$ to a root of $x^2-t$ in $E$, and the only root is $\alpha$ itself, so $\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$ is trivial and its fixed field is $E \ne F$. The failing property is **separability** (not normality).

**14.** **Strategy.** For the tower law, multiply bases and check spanning and independence; the corollaries are divisibility statements.

*Tower law.* Let $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$, let $(e_i)_{i\in I}$ be a $K$-basis of $E$ and $(f_j)_{j\in J}$ an $F$-basis of $K$. **Spanning:** if $x = \sum_i a_ie_i$ with $a_i \in K$ and $a_i = \sum_j b_{ij}f_j$ with $b_{ij}\in F$, then $x = \sum_{i,j}b_{ij}(e_if_j)$. **Independence:** if $\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}e_if_j = 0$ with $c_{ij}\in F$, then $\sum_i\big(\sum_j c_{ij}f_j\big)e_i = 0$ with $\sum_jc_{ij}f_j \in K$, so each inner sum is $0$ by $K$-independence of the $e_i$, and then each $c_{ij} = 0$ by $F$-independence of the $f_j$. Hence $(e_if_j)$ is an $F$-basis and $[E:F] = |I||J| = [E:K][K:F]$.

*Prime degree.* If $F \subseteq L \subseteq E$ then $[L:F]$ divides $p$ by Corollary 7.1.2, so $[L:F] \in \{1,p\}$, i.e. $L = F$ or $L = E$: no intermediate fields.

*Degree $4$.* If $E = F(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$ with $\deg m_\alpha = 4$, we are in the first case. Otherwise, take any $\alpha \in E\setminus F$; then $1 < [F(\alpha):F] < 4$ (the second inequality because $F(\alpha) \neq E$), and $[F(\alpha):F]$ divides $4$, so it equals $2$ and $F(\alpha)$ is the required intermediate field.

**15.** **Strategy.** Field axioms are inherited from $E$; then quote Artin and the automorphism bound.

Let $x,y \in E^H$ and $\sigma \in H$. Then $\sigma(x\pm y) = \sigma(x)\pm\sigma(y) = x\pm y$, $\sigma(xy) = xy$, $\sigma(1) = 1$, and for $x \ne 0$, $\sigma(x^{-1}) = \sigma(x)^{-1} = x^{-1}$. Since $\sigma(0) = 0$ and $\sigma(-x) = -x$, $E^H$ is closed under the field operations, so it is a subfield.

Now let $H$ be finite and $F = E^H$. Every $h \in H$ fixes $F$ by definition, so $H \le \operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$, hence $|H| \le |\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)|$. By Theorem 7.6.3, $|\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)| \le [E:F]$, and by Artin's theorem (7.6.4), $[E:F] = [E:E^H] = |H|$. Chaining,
$$|H| \le |\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)| \le [E:F] = |H|,$$
so $\operatorname{Aut}(E/F) = H$. Therefore $E^{\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)} = E^H = F$: the extension is Galois with group $H$.

**16.** **Strategy.** Irreducibility by Eisenstein, then find all roots inside the field generated by one root.

$f = x^4-4x^2+2$ is Eisenstein at $2$ ($2 \mid -4$, $2 \mid 2$, $4 \nmid 2$), hence irreducible, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ for any root $\alpha$.

The roots satisfy $x^2 = 2\pm\sqrt2$. Put $\alpha = \sqrt{2+\sqrt2}$ (real, positive) and $\beta = \sqrt{2-\sqrt2}$. Then
$$\alpha^2\beta^2 = (2+\sqrt2)(2-\sqrt2) = 2, \qquad \alpha\beta = \sqrt2,$$
so $\sqrt2 = \alpha^2-2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ and $\beta = \sqrt2/\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$. Hence all four roots $\pm\alpha, \pm\beta$ lie in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, which therefore is the splitting field $E$ and has degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Define $\sigma$ on $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ by $\sigma(\alpha) = \beta$. Since $\beta$ is a root of the minimal polynomial $f$ of $\alpha$, this extends to a $\mathbb{Q}$-automorphism (Theorem 7.2.3). Compute its action:
$$\sigma(\sqrt2) = \sigma(\alpha^2-2) = \beta^2-2 = -\sqrt2, \qquad \sigma(\beta) = \sigma\!\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{\alpha}\right) = \frac{-\sqrt2}{\beta} = -\alpha,$$
using $\sqrt2/\beta = \alpha$. Then $\sigma^2(\alpha) = -\alpha$, $\sigma^3(\alpha) = -\beta$, $\sigma^4(\alpha) = \alpha$, so $\sigma$ has order $4$; a group of order $4$ containing an element of order $4$ is $C_4$, so $\operatorname{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q}) \cong C_4$.

*Consistency checks.* The resolvent cubic is $y^3+4y^2-8y-32 = (y+4)(y^2-8)$, with exactly one rational root, so the group is $C_4$ or $D_4$ (Theorem 7.11.4); the computed order $[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ rules out $D_4$. Also $\Delta = 2048 = 2^{11}$ is not a square, consistent with $C_4 \not\subseteq A_4$ (a $4$-cycle is odd).

**17.** **Strategy.** Extend an automorphism of $K$ to $E$ using the extension lemma, then use normality of $E/F$ to land back in $E$.

Let $\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$. Regard $\sigma$ as an $F$-embedding $K \to \overline F$. By Lemma 7.4.2 it extends to an $F$-embedding $\tau : E \to \overline F$. Since $E/F$ is finite normal, Theorem 7.5.1(3) gives $\tau(E) = E$; hence $\tau \in \operatorname{Aut}(E/F) = G$. This proves that the restriction map
$$\rho : G \to \operatorname{Gal}(K/F), \qquad \rho(\tau) = \tau|_K$$
is surjective (provided $K/F$ is normal, so that $\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$ is the full automorphism group of $K$ over $F$; if $K/F$ is Galois this is automatic). Its kernel is $\{\tau \in G : \tau|_K = \operatorname{id}\} = \operatorname{Gal}(E/K)$, which is therefore normal in $G$ as the kernel of a homomorphism, and the first isomorphism theorem gives $G/\operatorname{Gal}(E/K) \cong \operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$.

**18.** **Strategy.** Identify $G$ with $(\mathbb{Z}/9)^\times$, find its subgroups, and use the fundamental theorem with the explicit action $\zeta_9 \mapsto \zeta_9^k$.

$G \cong (\mathbb{Z}/9)^\times = \{1,2,4,5,7,8\}$, cyclic of order $6$ generated by $2$: the powers of $2$ modulo $9$ are $2,4,8,7,5,1$. The subgroups are:
* $\{1\}$: fixed field $E = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9)$, degree $[G:1] = 6$;
* $\{1,8\}$ (order $2$; note $8 \equiv -1$): the corresponding automorphism is complex conjugation $\zeta_9\mapsto\zeta_9^{-1}$, whose fixed field is the real subfield $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9+\zeta_9^{-1})$, of degree $6/2 = 3$;
* $\{1,4,7\}$ (order $3$): these are the $k \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so they fix $\zeta_9^3 = \zeta_3$; the fixed field contains $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$ and has degree $6/3 = 2$, so it equals $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$;
* $G$: fixed field $\mathbb{Q}$, degree $1$.

The unique quadratic subfield is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, fixed by the unique subgroup of order $3$, namely $\{1,4,7\}$. **24.** **Strategy.** Multiplicativity comes from grouping embeddings by their restriction; the inequality and the separability criterion come by induction on $[E:F]$, counting the roots of the minimal polynomial of a chosen element.

*(Multiplicativity.)* Fix an algebraic closure $\overline F$ containing $E$. Every $F$-embedding $\tau : E \to \overline F$ restricts to an $F$-embedding $\sigma = \tau|_K : K \to \overline F$; conversely every such $\sigma$ extends to at least one $\tau$ by Lemma 7.4.2. For a fixed $\sigma$, the extensions $\tau$ correspond to $K$-embeddings of $E$ into $\overline F$ after twisting all coefficients by $\sigma$: the map $\tau \mapsto \sigma^{-1}\circ\tau$ is a bijection from $\{\tau : \tau|_K = \sigma\}$ to the set of $K$-embeddings of $E$. (Here $\sigma^{-1}$ is defined on $\sigma(K)$ and extended arbitrarily to $\overline F$; the composite fixes $K$ and is a field homomorphism into $\overline F$.) Hence each $\sigma$ has exactly $[E:K]_s$ extensions, and summing over the $[K:F]_s$ choices of $\sigma$ gives $[E:F]_s = [K:F]_s[E:K]_s$.

*(Inequality and criterion.)* Induct on $n = [E:F]$; for $n = 1$, $[E:F]_s = 1 = [E:F]$ and $E/F$ is separable. For $n > 1$ choose $\alpha \in E\setminus F$, let $p = m_{\alpha,F}$ and $d = \deg p = [F(\alpha):F]$, and let $r$ be the number of distinct roots of $p$ in $\overline F$. An $F$-embedding of $F(\alpha)$ is determined by $\alpha \mapsto \rho$ for a root $\rho$ of $p$, and distinct roots give distinct embeddings, so $[F(\alpha):F]_s = r \le d = [F(\alpha):F]$, with equality iff $p$ is separable. By multiplicativity and the induction hypothesis applied to $E/F(\alpha)$,
$$[E:F]_s = [F(\alpha):F]_s\,[E:F(\alpha)]_s = r\cdot[E:F(\alpha)]_s \le d\cdot[E:F(\alpha)] = [E:F].$$
If $E/F$ is separable then $p$ is separable (so $r = d$) and $E/F(\alpha)$ is separable (so by induction $[E:F(\alpha)]_s = [E:F(\alpha)]$), giving equality. Conversely, if $[E:F]_s = [E:F]$, then for every $\alpha \in E\setminus F$ the displayed chain of equalities and inequalities must be tight, so $r = d$; thus every minimal polynomial of an element of $E$ has $\deg p$ distinct roots in $\overline F$, i.e. $E/F$ is separable.

**25.** **Strategy.** Extend automorphisms using normality, then apply the first isomorphism theorem.

Let $H \trianglelefteq G$ and $K = E^H$. First, $K/F$ is normal: by Theorem 7.5.1(3) it suffices to show that $\sigma(K) = K$ for every $F$-embedding $\sigma : K \to \overline F$. Extend $\sigma$ to $\tau \in G$ (Lemma 7.4.2 plus normality of $E/F$). For $x \in K$ and $h \in H$,
$$h(\tau(x)) = \tau\big(\tau^{-1}h\tau(x)\big) = \tau(x)$$
because $\tau^{-1}h\tau \in H$ (normality) and $x \in E^H$. So $\tau(x) \in E^H = K$, i.e. $\tau(K)\subseteq K$; as $K/F$ is finite, $\tau(K) = K$. Hence $K/F$ is normal, and separable because $E/F$ is separable (Theorem 7.7.1(3)) and separability is inherited by subextensions; so $K/F$ is Galois.

**32.** **Strategy.** For the forward direction, refine each abelian quotient using that finite abelian groups have chains with cyclic prime quotients; for the converse there is nothing to prove.

*(Refinement of an abelian group.)* Let $A$ be a finite abelian group, $A \ne \{e\}$. Choose $x \in A$ of prime order $p$ (Cauchy's theorem). By induction on $|A|$ applied to $A/\langle x\rangle$, there is a chain
$$\{e\} = B_0 \subseteq B_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq B_t = A/\langle x\rangle$$
with cyclic quotients of prime order. Let $\pi : A \to A/\langle x\rangle$ be the quotient map and set $C_j := \pi^{-1}(B_j)$. Then $C_0 = \langle x\rangle$, $C_t = A$, each $C_j \trianglelefteq C_{j+1}$ (preimages of normal subgroups), and $C_{j+1}/C_j \cong B_{j+1}/B_j$ is cyclic of prime order. Prepending $\{e\} \subseteq \langle x\rangle$ (a cyclic group of prime order) gives a chain for $A$ with cyclic prime quotients. Note that all subgroups of $A$ are normal because $A$ is abelian, which is what makes the refinement compatible with the ambient group.

*(Forward direction.)* Let $G = G_0 \trianglerighteq G_1 \trianglerighteq \cdots \trianglerighteq G_m = \{e\}$ with $A_i := G_i/G_{i+1}$ abelian. Refine each step: by the previous paragraph applied to $A_i$, there are subgroups $G_i = H_{i,0} \trianglerighteq H_{i,1} \trianglerighteq \cdots \trianglerighteq H_{i,t_i} = G_{i+1}$ with cyclic prime quotients. Concatenating the refinements gives a chain from $G$ to $\{e\}$ with all quotients cyclic of prime order.

*(Converse.)* A cyclic group of prime order is abelian, so a chain with cyclic prime quotients is in particular a chain with abelian quotients; hence $G$ is solvable.

**33.** **Strategy.** Use the class equation for the centre and induction; then compute commutators in the affine group.

*$p$-groups are solvable.* Let $G$ be a finite group of order $p^n$. For $n = 0$ this is trivial. For $n \ge 1$, the class equation
$$|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_{\text{nontrivial classes}} [G : C_G(x)]$$
has all indices $[G:C_G(x)]$ divisible by $p$, so $p \mid |Z(G)|$ and $Z(G) \ne \{e\}$. If $Z(G) = G$ then $G$ is abelian and solvable. Otherwise $Z(G)$ is a nontrivial abelian normal subgroup and $G/Z(G)$ is a $p$-group of smaller order, hence solvable by induction; so by Lemma 7.14.2(4) (an extension of a solvable group by a solvable group is solvable), $G$ is solvable.

*The affine group.* Let $G = \{(a,b) : a \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times,\ b\in\mathbb{F}_p\}$ with $(a,b)(a',b') = (aa', ab'+b)$; this is $C_p \rtimes C_{p-1}$ acting by $x\mapsto ax+b$. The translation subgroup $T = \{(1,b)\} \cong C_p$ is normal with $G/T \cong \mathbb{F}_p^\times \cong C_{p-1}$ abelian, so $G' \subseteq T$. For $a \ne 1$ and $b \ne 0$, using $(a,b)(a',b') = (aa', ab'+b)$,
$$(a,0)(1,b)(a,0)^{-1}(1,b)^{-1} = (a,ab)(a^{-1},0)(1,-b) = (1,ab)(1,-b) = (1,\ b(a-1)),$$
a nontrivial translation. Hence $T \subseteq G'$, so $G' = T \cong C_p$ and $G'' = \{e\}$. The derived series is $G \trianglerighteq C_p \trianglerighteq \{e\}$ with abelian quotients $C_{p-1}$ and $C_p$; $G$ is solvable.

*Application to $x^p-2$.* By Exercise 35, $\operatorname{Gal}(x^p-2/\mathbb{Q}) \cong C_p \rtimes C_{p-1}$, which is solvable. Adjoining $\zeta_p$ is a radical step ($\zeta_p^p = 1$), and over $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ the hypothesis of Theorem 7.14.8 is satisfied; hence $x^p-2$ is solvable by radicals over $\mathbb{Q}$.

**34.** **Strategy.** Irreducibility is the hard part: rule out linear and quadratic factors using reduction mod $2$ together with evaluations at $0,1,-1$. Then Dedekind mod $2$ gives a transposition and irreducibility gives a $5$-cycle.

Let $f = x^5-x-1$.

*No linear factor.* The only rational roots must be $\pm1$, and $f(1) = 1-1-1 = -1$, $f(-1) = -1+1-1 = -1$.

*Reduction mod $2$.* Over $\mathbb{F}_2$,
$$x^5+x+1 = (x^2+x+1)(x^3+x^2+1),$$
as one checks by expanding: $x^5+x^4+x^2+x^4+x^3+x+x^3+x^2+1 = x^5+x+1$. Both factors are irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_2$: $x^2+x+1$ has no root, and $x^3+x^2+1$ has values $1, 1$ at $0,1$, so it has no root and, being cubic, is irreducible.

*No quadratic factor.* Suppose $f = gh$ with $g,h\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ monic of degrees $2,3$. Reducing mod $2$ and using unique factorisation in $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$, we may assume
$$\bar g = x^2+x+1.$$
Write $g = x^2+bx+c$ with $b,c \in \mathbb{Z}$. From $f(0) = -1 = g(0)h(0) = c\,h(0)$ we get $c = \pm1$.

If $c = 1$, then $g(1) = b+2$ and $g(-1) = 2-b$. Since $f(1) = f(-1) = -1$, both $g(1)$ and $g(-1)$ are $\pm1$. But $b+2 = \pm1$ gives $b\in\{-1,-3\}$, while $2-b = \pm1$ gives $b \in \{1,3\}$: no common value, contradiction.

If $c = -1$, then $g(1) = b$ and $g(-1) = -b$, both $\pm1$; this is consistent, and $g = x^2+x-1$ or $x^2-x-1$ (both congruent to $x^2+x+1$ mod $2$). Rule them out by division with remainder:
$$x^5-x-1 = (x^2+x-1)(x^3-x^2+2x-3) + (4x-4), \qquad x^5-x-1 = (x^2-x-1)(x^3+x^2+2x+3) + (4x+2),$$
and the remainders $4x-4$, $4x+2$ are nonzero. (Equivalently: if $\alpha^2 = 1-\alpha$ then $\alpha^5 = 5\alpha-3$, so $f(\alpha) = 4\alpha-4 = 4(\alpha-1) \ne 0$ since $g(1) = 1 \ne 0$; if $\alpha^2 = \alpha+1$ then $\alpha^5 = 5\alpha+3$ and $f(\alpha) = 4\alpha+2 \ne 0$ since $\alpha = -\frac12$ is not a root of $x^2-x-1$.)

Hence $f$ has no factor of degree $1$ or $2$, and since $\deg f = 5$ it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

*A transposition.* The reduction $\bar f = x^5+x+1$ is separable: $\bar f' = 5x^4+1 = x^4+1 = (x+1)^4$ in characteristic $2$, and $\bar f(1) = 1 \ne 0$, so $\gcd(\bar f,\bar f') = 1$. By Dedekind's theorem (Theorem 7.8.2) with the factorisation of degrees $2$ and $3$, $G = \operatorname{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q})$ contains a permutation of cycle type $(2,3)$, which is a transposition.

*Conclusion.* Irreducibility makes $G$ transitive on the $5$ roots (Lemma 5.8.2), so $5 \mid |G|$ and, by Cauchy's theorem, $G$ contains an element of order $5$, i.e. a $5$-cycle. By Lemma 7.8.4, a transitive subgroup of $S_5$ containing a transposition is $S_5$. Hence $G \cong S_5$, and $f$ is not solvable by radicals (Theorem 7.14.6).

**35.** **Strategy.** Compute the degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha,\zeta_p)$ by intersecting the two obvious subfields, count automorphisms, and identify the semidirect structure; then use the discriminant for the parity question.

Let $p$ be prime, $\alpha = \sqrt[p]{2}$, $E = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha,\zeta_p)$, $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, $L = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$.

*Degree.* $[K:\mathbb{Q}] = p$ since $x^p-2$ is Eisenstein at $2$. Since $p$ is prime, $K$ has no intermediate fields (Corollary 7.1.3), so $K\cap L$ is $\mathbb{Q}$ or $K$; but $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and $L \not\subseteq \mathbb{R}$ (for $p \ge 3$), so $K \cap L = \mathbb{Q}$. As $L/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois, $[KL:L] = [K:K\cap L] = p$ (standard: for $L/F$ Galois, $[KL:L] = [K:K\cap L]$). Hence
$$[E:\mathbb{Q}] = [L:\mathbb{Q}]\,[E:L] = (p-1)\cdot p.$$

*Automorphisms.* For $a \in \{0,\dots,p-1\}$ and $b \in \{1,\dots,p-1\}$, the assignment
$$\alpha \mapsto \zeta_p^a\alpha, \qquad \zeta_p \mapsto \zeta_p^b$$
extends to a field homomorphism $E \to \mathbb{C}$ (the images satisfy the defining relations $(\zeta_p^a\alpha)^p = 2$ and $(\zeta_p^b)^p = 1$), and it is injective; since there are $p(p-1) = [E:\mathbb{Q}]$ such maps, each is an automorphism of $E$. Denote it $\sigma_{a,b}$.

*Structure.* Let $H = \{\sigma_{a,1}\} \cong C_p$ (indeed $\sigma_{a,1}^k = \sigma_{ka,1}$ and $\sigma_{1,1}$ has order $p$); the map $G \to (\mathbb{Z}/p)^\times$, $\sigma_{a,b}\mapsto b$, is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $H$, so $H \trianglelefteq G$ and $G/H \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p)^\times$. The subgroup $\{\sigma_{0,b}\} \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p)^\times$ is a complement ($H \cap \{\sigma_{0,b}\} = \{1\}$, $G = H\{\sigma_{0,b}\}$). The conjugation action is $\sigma_{0,b}\sigma_{a,1}\sigma_{0,b}^{-1} = \sigma_{ab,1}$, i.e. multiplication of $a$ by $b$, so
$$G \cong C_p \rtimes (\mathbb{Z}/p)^\times,$$
the group of affine maps $x\mapsto bx+a$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$.

*Containment in $A_p$.* For a separable $f$ of degree $n$, $G\subseteq A_n$ iff $\Delta(f)$ is a square in $\mathbb{Q}$ (Theorem 7.10.3). The discriminant of $x^p-a$ is the standard
$$\Delta(x^p-a) = (-1)^{p(p-1)/2} p^p a^{p-1}.$$
With $a = 2$,
$$\Delta(x^p-2) = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}\, p^p\, 2^{p-1} = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}\, p \cdot \big(p^{(p-1)/2}2^{(p-1)/2}\big)^2,$$
using $p^p = p\,(p^{(p-1)/2})^2$. Since $\pm p$ is not a square in $\mathbb{Q}$, $\Delta$ is never a square; hence $G \not\subseteq A_p$ for every odd prime $p$.

*Checks.* $p = 3$: $G \cong C_3\rtimes C_2 \cong S_3$ of order $6$, and $\Delta(x^3-2) = -108 = -3\cdot 36$, not a square — consistent with $S_3 \not\subseteq A_3$. $p = 5$: $|G| = 20$ and $\Delta(x^5-2) = 5^5\cdot 2^4 = 50000 = 5\cdot 100^2$, not a square; indeed $A_5$ has no subgroup of order $20$, so $G \not\subseteq A_5$.

**36.** **Strategy.** Refine the solvable chain to prime cyclic steps, pass to fixed fields by the fundamental theorem, and extract radicals by Kummer's lemma (Lagrange resolvents).

*Kummer's lemma, complete proof.* Let $F$ contain a primitive $p$-th root of unity $\zeta$, with $p$ prime and $\operatorname{char} F \ne p$; let $K/F$ be cyclic Galois of degree $p$ and $\sigma$ a generator. Choose $\alpha$ with $K = F(\alpha)$ (a primitive element exists: $K/F$ is finite separable, and one may take $\alpha$ to generate; or simply pick $\alpha \in K\setminus F$, which generates since $[K:F] = p$ is prime). Define
$$\beta := \sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\zeta^{-k}\sigma^k(\alpha).$$
Then
$$\sigma(\beta) = \sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\zeta^{-k}\sigma^{k+1}(\alpha) = \zeta\sum_{j=1}^{p}\zeta^{-j}\sigma^{j}(\alpha) = \zeta\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\zeta^{-j}\sigma^{j}(\alpha) = \zeta\beta,$$
where we used $\sigma^p(\alpha) = \alpha$ and $\zeta^{-p} = 1$. Hence $\sigma(\beta^p) = (\zeta\beta)^p = \beta^p$, so $\beta^p \in K^{\langle\sigma\rangle} = F$. Moreover $\beta \ne 0$: otherwise $\sum_k \zeta^{-k}\sigma^k(\alpha) = 0$ is a nontrivial linear relation over $K$ among the distinct characters $\operatorname{id},\sigma,\dots,\sigma^{p-1}$, contradicting Dedekind's lemma (7.6.2). Finally $F(\beta) \ne F$ (as $\beta \ne 0$ and $\sigma(\beta) \ne \beta$), so $F(\beta) = K$ because $[K:F] = p$ is prime.

*The proof of the theorem.* Let $E$ be the splitting field of $f$, $G = \operatorname{Gal}(E/F)$, and suppose $F$ contains a primitive $p$-th root of unity for every prime $p \mid |G|$. By Exercise 32, $G$ has a chain
$$G = G_0 \trianglerighteq G_1 \trianglerighteq \cdots \trianglerighteq G_m = \{1\}$$
with $G_{i-1}/G_i$ cyclic of prime order $p_i$. Put $K_i := E^{G_i}$. By the fundamental theorem, $F = K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_m = E$, each $K_i/K_{i-1}$ is Galois with $\operatorname{Gal}(K_i/K_{i-1}) \cong G_{i-1}/G_i$ cyclic of order $p_i$ (Theorem 7.9.1(c), applied to the Galois extension $E/K_{i-1}$ and the normal subgroup $G_i$).

Since $p_i \mid |G|$, the hypothesis gives $\zeta_{p_i} \in F \subseteq K_{i-1}$. Kummer's lemma produces $\beta_i$ with $K_i = K_{i-1}(\beta_i)$ and $\beta_i^{p_i} \in K_{i-1}$. Therefore $E/F$ is a radical extension, and the splitting field of $f$ is contained in it; so $f$ is solvable by radicals. (If $F$ does not contain the required roots of unity, adjoin them first: each $\zeta_p$ satisfies $\zeta_p^p = 1$, so this is a radical step, and then apply the argument over the larger field.)

**37.** **Strategy.** Compare the degree $[F(x_1,\dots,x_n):F(e_1,\dots,e_n)]$ with $|S_n|$ using the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials, then apply Artin.

Let $F$ be a field, $L = F(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ the field of rational functions in $n$ indeterminates, $e_1,\dots,e_n$ the elementary symmetric polynomials, and $K = F(e_1,\dots,e_n)$. Each $e_i$ is fixed by every permutation of the variables, so $K \subseteq L^{S_n}$. By Artin's theorem (7.6.4) applied to the group $S_n$ of automorphisms of $L$,
$$[L : L^{S_n}] = |S_n| = n!.$$

*Claim: $[L:K] = n!$.* By the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials (proved by induction on $n$ using the lexicographic division algorithm in $F[x_1,\dots,x_n]$; see Rings §7.13 for the polynomial case), every symmetric polynomial in $x_1,\dots,x_n$ is a polynomial in $e_1,\dots,e_n$. More precisely, the monomials
$$x_1^{a_1}x_2^{a_2}\cdots x_n^{a_n}, \qquad 0 \le a_i < i,$$
form a basis of $F[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ as a module over $F[e_1,\dots,e_n]$; there are $1\cdot2\cdots n = n!$ of them. Localising at the nonzero elements of $F[e_1,\dots,e_n]$ (a flat, hence exact, operation) shows that the same $n!$ monomials form a basis of $L$ over $K$. Hence $[L:K] = n!$.

Now $K \subseteq L^{S_n} \subseteq L$, and $[L:K] = n! = [L:L^{S_n}]$; by the tower law, $[L:K] = [L:L^{S_n}]\,[L^{S_n}:K]$, so $[L^{S_n}:K] = 1$ and $L^{S_n} = K$. This is the theorem: **the fixed field of $S_n$ is the field of symmetric rational functions.**

*Application to the general quintic.* Let $t_1,\dots,t_5$ be indeterminates and $F = \mathbb{Q}(t_1,\dots,t_5)$, identified with $\mathbb{Q}(e_1,\dots,e_5)$ inside $L = \mathbb{Q}(x_1,\dots,x_5)$. The polynomial
$$f(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{5}(x-x_i) = x^5-t_1x^4+t_2x^3-t_3x^2+t_4x-t_5 \in F[x]$$
has splitting field $L$ over $F$ (its roots are the $x_i$, which generate $L$). Since $L^{S_5} = F$, Corollary 7.6.5 gives that $L/F$ is Galois with group $S_5$. Hence $\operatorname{Gal}(f/F) \cong S_5$, which is not solvable (Theorem 7.15.1); by Theorem 7.14.6 there is no formula for the roots of the general quintic in terms of its coefficients using field operations and root extractions.

**38.** **Strategy.** Complex conjugation is the automorphism $\sigma_{-1}$; compute its fixed field, degrees and quotient group; then identify the three examples.

Let $n \ge 3$, $E = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$, $G \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$ (Theorem 7.12.5), and let $c$ denote complex conjugation, which acts as $c(\zeta_n) = \zeta_n^{-1} = \zeta_n^{n-1}$, i.e. $c = \sigma_{-1}$. Since $n \ge 3$, $\zeta_n \ne \zeta_n^{-1}$, so $c \ne \operatorname{id}$ and $c^2 = \operatorname{id}$; thus $\langle c\rangle$ is a subgroup of order $2$.

*Fixed field.* Every element of $E^+ := \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1})$ is fixed by $c$. Conversely, if $x \in E$ is fixed by $c$, then $x \in E^{\langle c\rangle}$, and by the fundamental theorem $[E : E^{\langle c\rangle}] = |\langle c\rangle| = 2$; since $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1}) \subseteq E^{\langle c\rangle}$ and $[E:\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1})] = 2$ (because $\zeta_n$ satisfies the quadratic $X^2-(\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1})X+1$ over $E^+$ and does not lie in $E^+$, which is real while $\zeta_n$ is not), the two degree-$2$ fields coincide:
$$E^+ = E^{\langle c\rangle}, \qquad [E:E^+] = 2, \qquad [E^+:\mathbb{Q}] = \frac{\varphi(n)}{2}.$$

*Galois group.* $G$ is abelian, so every subgroup is normal; in particular $\langle c\rangle \trianglelefteq G$. By Theorem 7.9.1(c), $E^+/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois and
$$\operatorname{Gal}(E^+/\mathbb{Q}) \cong G/\langle c\rangle \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times/\{\pm1\}.$$

*Examples.*
* $n = 7$: $\varphi(7)/2 = 3$, and $E^+ = \mathbb{Q}(2\cos\frac{2\pi}{7})$ has degree $3$. For $\eta = \zeta_7+\zeta_7^{-1}$, dividing $1+\zeta_7+\cdots+\zeta_7^6 = 0$ by $\zeta_7^3$ gives $(\zeta_7^3+\zeta_7^{-3})+(\zeta_7^2+\zeta_7^{-2})+(\zeta_7+\zeta_7^{-1})+1 = 0$, and with $\zeta_7+\zeta_7^{-1} = \eta$, $\zeta_7^2+\zeta_7^{-2} = \eta^2-2$, $\zeta_7^3+\zeta_7^{-3} = \eta^3-3\eta$ this becomes $\eta^3+\eta^2-2\eta-1 = 0$; the cubic is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational root), so it is the minimal polynomial.
* $n = 8$: $\varphi(8)/2 = 2$ and $E^+ = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ (Exercise 30).
* $n = 9$: $\varphi(9)/2 = 3$ and $E^+ = \mathbb{Q}(2\cos\frac{2\pi}{9})$, whose minimal polynomial is $x^3-3x+1$ (Example 9.4).
