## Intermediate (13–22)

**13.** Apply the power-of-a-product and power-of-a-power laws:

$$
\left(x^3y^2\right)^{-2}=x^{-6}y^{-4}, \qquad \left(xy^3\right)^4=x^4y^{12}.
$$

Multiply and add exponents:

$$
x^{-6}y^{-4}\cdot x^4y^{12}=x^{-6+4}y^{-4+12}=x^{-2}y^8=\frac{y^8}{x^2},
$$

with $x,y\neq0$.

**14.** Add the exponents in the numerator, then subtract the denominator's exponent:

$$
\frac{a^{1/2}a^{1/3}}{a^{1/6}}=a^{1/2+1/3-1/6}.
$$

Use a common denominator of $6$:

$$
\frac12+\frac13-\frac16=\frac36+\frac26-\frac16=\frac46=\frac23.
$$

So the expression equals $a^{2/3}$, with $a>0$.

**15.** (a) $81^{3/4}=\left(\sqrt[4]{81}\right)^3=3^3=27$.

(b) $125^{-2/3}=\dfrac{1}{125^{2/3}}=\dfrac{1}{\left(\sqrt[3]{125}\right)^2}=\dfrac{1}{5^2}=\dfrac1{25}$.

(c) $\left(\dfrac{9}{16}\right)^{3/2}=\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{9}{16}}\right)^3=\left(\dfrac34\right)^3=\dfrac{27}{64}$.

**16.** Factor out perfect squares:

$$
72x^4y^7=36\cdot2\cdot x^4\cdot y^6\cdot y.
$$

Therefore, for $x\ge0$ and $y\ge0$,

$$
\sqrt{72x^4y^7}=\sqrt{36}\,x^2\,y^3\,\sqrt{2y}=6x^2y^3\sqrt{2y}.
$$

Because $y\ge0$, the factor $y^3$ is nonnegative, so no absolute value is needed.

**21.** (a) $2^x=2^7$ implies $x=7$, because powers with the same base are equal only when the exponents are equal.

## Advanced (23–31)

**23.** *Strategy:* write $a^m$ as $a^n\cdot a^{m-n}$ and divide by $a^n$.

Since $m>n$, the exponent $m-n$ is a natural number. By the product law,

$$
a^n a^{m-n}=a^{n+(m-n)}=a^m.
$$

Because $a\neq0$, division by $a^n$ is valid, and

$$
\frac{a^m}{a^n}=\frac{a^n a^{m-n}}{a^n}=a^{m-n}.
$$

If $m=n$, then numerator and denominator are the same nonzero number, so the quotient is $1=a^0$. If $m<n$, write $n=m+k$ with $k>0$. Then the first case applied to $a^n/a^m$ gives $a^k$, so

$$
\frac{a^m}{a^n}=\frac{1}{a^{n-m}}=a^{-(n-m)}.
$$

Thus the quotient law extends consistently to all integer exponents.

**24.** *Proof of $(ab)^n=a^n b^n$ by induction on $n$.* For $n=1$, $(ab)^1=ab=a^1b^1$. Assume the identity for $n=k$. Then

$$
(ab)^{k+1}=(ab)^k(ab)=(a^kb^k)(ab)=(a^ka)(b^kb)=a^{k+1}b^{k+1},
$$

using associativity and commutativity of multiplication. By induction, the identity holds for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

*Proof of $(a^m)^n=a^{mn}$ by induction on $n$.* For $n=1$, $(a^m)^1=a^m=a^{m\cdot1}$. Assume $(a^m)^k=a^{mk}$. Then

$$
(a^m)^{k+1}=(a^m)^k a^m=a^{mk}a^m=a^{mk+m}=a^{m(k+1)},
$$

using the product law. By induction, the identity holds for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

**25.** *Strategy:* contradiction, with the fraction in lowest terms.

Suppose $\sqrt2$ is rational. Then $\sqrt2=p/q$ for integers $p,q$ with $q\neq0$. Choose the representation in lowest terms, so $\gcd(p,q)=1$. Squaring gives

$$
2=\frac{p^2}{q^2}, \qquad p^2=2q^2.
$$

Thus $p^2$ is even. If $p$ were odd, say $p=2k+1$, then $p^2=4k^2+4k+1$ would be odd; hence $p$ is even. Write $p=2r$. Substituting,

$$
4r^2=2q^2, \qquad q^2=2r^2.
$$

So $q^2$ is even, and the same parity argument shows $q$ is even. Therefore $2$ divides both $p$ and $q$, contradicting $\gcd(p,q)=1$. The assumption is false, so $\sqrt2$ is irrational. $\square$

**26.** *Strategy:* copy the parity argument, replacing "even" by "divisible by $3$".

Suppose $\sqrt3=a/b$ with integers $a,b$, $b\neq0$, and $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Squaring gives $a^2=3b^2$, so $3\mid a^2$.

If $a$ is not divisible by $3$, then $a=3k+1$ or $a=3k+2$. In the first case $a^2=9k^2+6k+1\equiv1\pmod3$; in the second, $a^2=9k^2+12k+4\equiv1\pmod3$. Neither is divisible by $3$. Hence $3\mid a$. Write $a=3c$. Then

$$
9c^2=3b^2, \qquad b^2=3c^2,
$$

so $3\mid b^2$, and the same argument gives $3\mid b$. Thus $3$ divides both $a$ and $b$, contradicting $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Therefore $\sqrt3$ is irrational. $\square$

\left(64x^{-6}y^9\right)^{-1/3}=64^{-1/3}x^{2}y^{-3}=\frac{x^2}{4y^3}.

The exponents multiply: $(-6)\left(-\frac13\right)=2$ and $9\left(-\frac13\right)=-3$; and $64^{-1/3}=1/\sqrt[3]{64}=1/4$. The variables satisfy $x,y\neq0$.

**28.** Factor out perfect cubes:

16x^5y^8=8\cdot2\cdot x^3\cdot x^2\cdot y^6\cdot y^2.

Therefore

\sqrt[3]{16x^5y^8}=2xy^2\sqrt[3]{2x^2y^2}.

**29.** Newton's iteration is $x_{k+1}=\frac12\left(x_k+\frac{2}{x_k}\right)$. Starting from $x_0=1.5=\frac32$:

$$
x_1=\frac12\left(\frac32+\frac{4}{3}\right)
=\frac12\cdot\frac{17}{6}=\frac{17}{12}\approx1.4166667,
$$

$$
x_2=\frac12\left(\frac{17}{12}+\frac{24}{17}\right)
=\frac12\cdot\frac{289+288}{204}
=\frac{577}{408}\approx1.4142157,
$$

$$
x_3=\frac12\left(\frac{577}{408}+\frac{816}{577}\right)
=\frac12\cdot\frac{332929+332928}{235416}
=\frac{665857}{470832}\approx1.4142136.
$$

The true value is $\sqrt2\approx1.414213562$, so to three decimal places $\sqrt2\approx1.414$. Each iterate roughly doubles the number of correct digits.

**30.** (a) Multiply the distance per light-year by the number of light-years:

$$
4.2\times9.46\times10^{12}=39.732\times10^{12}=3.9732\times10^{13}\text{ km}.
$$

So the star is about $3.97\times10^{13}$ km away.

(b) Time equals distance divided by speed:

$$
\frac{3.9732\times10^{13}}{1.0\times10^{5}}=3.9732\times10^{8}\text{ hours}.
$$

Divide by $8766$ hours per year:

$$
\frac{3.9732\times10^{8}}{8766}\approx4.53\times10^{4}\text{ years}.

The journey would take about $45{,}300$ years. The calculation assumes the speed is constant and ignores relativistic effects, so it is an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a physical travel plan.

**31.** (a) $\sqrt{x+7}=5$. The left side is nonnegative, and $5>0$, so squaring is safe:

$$
x+7=25, \qquad x=18.
$$

Check: $\sqrt{18+7}=\sqrt{25}=5$.

(b) $x=\sqrt{x+6}$. Since the right side is a principal square root, it is nonnegative; hence any solution must satisfy $x\ge0$. Square:

$$
x^2=x+6, \qquad x^2-x-6=0, \qquad (x-3)(x+2)=0.
$$

The candidates are $x=3$ and $x=-2$. The candidate $x=-2$ fails the requirement $x\ge0$; indeed, substituting gives $-2=\sqrt4=2$, which is false. The only solution is $x=3$. Check: $\sqrt{3+6}=\sqrt9=3$.

## Challenge (32–36)

**32.** *Strategy:* suppose the sum is rational, square it, and isolate a known irrational number.

Suppose $r=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ is rational. Squaring,

$$
r^2=2+2\sqrt6+3=5+2\sqrt6,
$$

so

$$
\sqrt6=\frac{r^2-5}{2}.
$$

The right side is a rational number, because $r$ is rational. Hence $\sqrt6$ would be rational. But $6$ is not a perfect square, so $\sqrt6$ is irrational by the corollary in Section 7.10. This contradiction shows that $r$ cannot be rational. Therefore $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ is irrational. $\square$

**33.** *Strategy:* substitute $u=2^x$, which turns the equation into a quadratic.

Since $2^{2x}=(2^x)^2$, let $u=2^x$. Because $2^x>0$ for every real $x$, only positive $u$ are admissible. The equation becomes

$$
u^2-5u+4=0.
$$

Factor:

$$
(u-1)(u-4)=0,
$$

so $u=1$ or $u=4$; both are positive. Return to $x$:

$$
2^x=1 \implies x=0, \qquad 2^x=4=2^2 \implies x=2.
$$

Check: for $x=0$, $2^0-5\cdot2^0+4=1-5+4=0$. For $x=2$, $2^4-5\cdot2^2+4=16-20+4=0$. Both solutions are valid.

**34.** *Strategy:* contradiction and parity, as for $\sqrt2$, but with cubes.

Suppose $\sqrt[3]{2}=a/b$ with positive integers $a,b$ and $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Cubing gives

$$
2=\frac{a^3}{b^3}, \qquad a^3=2b^3.
$$

Thus $a^3$ is even. If $a$ were odd, then $a^3$ would be odd, so $a$ is even; write $a=2c$. Substituting,

$$
8c^3=2b^3, \qquad b^3=4c^3.
$$

The right side is even, so $b^3$ is even, and therefore $b$ is even. Thus $2$ divides both $a$ and $b$, contradicting $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Hence $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is irrational. $\square$

*Alternative view:* in the prime factorization of a perfect cube, the exponent of every prime is a multiple of $3$. In $a^3$ the exponent of the prime $2$ is a multiple of $3$, while in $2b^3$ it is one more than a multiple of $3$. This is impossible by unique prime factorization.

**35.** *Strategy:* compare the squares. Both sums are positive, so squaring preserves the order.

Let $A=\sqrt7+\sqrt{10}$ and $B=\sqrt3+\sqrt{14}$. Then

$$
A^2=7+2\sqrt{70}+10=17+2\sqrt{70},
$$

$$
B^2=3+2\sqrt{42}+14=17+2\sqrt{42}.
$$

Since $70>42$, we have $\sqrt{70}>\sqrt{42}$, hence $A^2>B^2$. Because $A$ and $B$ are positive, $A>B$. Therefore

$$
\sqrt7+\sqrt{10}>\sqrt3+\sqrt{14}.
$$

**36.** *Strategy:* prove the contrapositive. If $n$ is composite, then $2^n-1$ factors.

Suppose $n=ab$ with integers $a,b\ge2$. Using the identity $x^b-1=(x-1)(x^{b-1}+x^{b-2}+\cdots+x+1)$ with $x=2^a$,

$$
2^n-1=2^{ab}-1=\left(2^a\right)^b-1
=\left(2^a-1\right)\left(\left(2^a\right)^{b-1}+\left(2^a\right)^{b-2}+\cdots+2^a+1\right).
$$

Both factors are integers greater than $1$. Indeed, $2^a-1\ge2^2-1=3$, and the second factor has $b\ge2$ positive terms, so it is at least $2^a+1>1$. Hence $2^n-1$ is composite.

Therefore, if $2^n-1$ is prime, $n$ cannot be composite. Since $n\ge2$, $n$ must be prime. $\square$

The converse is false: $n=11$ is prime, but $2^{11}-1=2047=23\cdot89$ is not prime. The result gives a necessary condition for a Mersenne prime, not a sufficient one.
