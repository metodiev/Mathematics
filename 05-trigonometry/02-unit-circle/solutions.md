# The Unit Circle and Radian Measure — Solutions

> [Tutorial](README.md) · [Exercises](exercises.md)

Each solution states the strategy, then the steps. Conversions and exact values are given in closed form; decimals appear only where the problem asks for them, marked with $\approx$. If an answer disagrees with yours, check first whether you used radians where radians are required — that is the usual cause.

## Beginner

**1.** Multiply each angle by $\pi/180$:

$$60^\circ = \frac{60\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{3}, \qquad 135^\circ = \frac{135\pi}{180} = \frac{3\pi}{4},$$
$$210^\circ = \frac{210\pi}{180} = \frac{7\pi}{6}, \qquad 330^\circ = \frac{330\pi}{180} = \frac{11\pi}{6}.$$

Each fraction is reduced by dividing numerator and denominator by $15$ or $30$. *Check:* $330^\circ$ is $30^\circ$ short of a full turn, so its radian measure must be $2\pi - \pi/6 = 11\pi/6$.

**2.** Multiply each angle by $180/\pi$; the $\pi$ cancels:

$$\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{180^\circ}{3} = 60^\circ, \qquad \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{3 \cdot 180^\circ}{4} = 135^\circ,$$
$$\frac{5\pi}{3} = \frac{5 \cdot 180^\circ}{3} = 300^\circ, \qquad \frac{11\pi}{6} = \frac{11 \cdot 180^\circ}{6} = 330^\circ.$$

**3.** With $\theta$ already in radians, Property 8.2 applies directly:

$$s = r\theta = 4 \cdot \frac{3\pi}{4} = 3\pi \approx 9.42.$$

*Check:* the whole circumference is $8\pi \approx 25.13$, and $3\pi$ is $\frac38$ of it, matching $\frac{3\pi/4}{2\pi} = \frac38$.

**4.**

$$A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta = \tfrac12 \cdot 25 \cdot \frac{2\pi}{5} = \frac{25\pi}{5} = 5\pi \approx 15.71.$$

*Check:* the disc area is $25\pi$, and the sector is $\frac{2\pi/5}{2\pi} = \frac15$ of the disc, so its area is $5\pi$, as found.

**5.** Read each from the exact-value table of the tutorial:

$$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}, \qquad \tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \sqrt3,$$
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1, \qquad \cos\pi = -1, \qquad \tan\frac{3\pi}{4} = -1.$$

The last one: $3\pi/4$ is in quadrant II, where tangent is negative, and its reference angle is $\pi/4$ with $\tan\frac{\pi}{4} = 1$.

**6.** Reduce to $[0, 2\pi)$ and compare with the quadrant boundaries $\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}$:

* $\frac{5\pi}{6}$: since $\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{3\pi}{6} < \frac{5\pi}{6} < \pi$, it is in quadrant **II**.
* $\frac{4\pi}{3}$: since $\pi = \frac{3\pi}{3} < \frac{4\pi}{3} < \frac{3\pi}{2} = \frac{9\pi}{6}$, it is in quadrant **III**.
* $\frac{7\pi}{4}$: since $\frac{3\pi}{2} = \frac{6\pi}{4} < \frac{7\pi}{4} < 2\pi$, it is in quadrant **IV**.
* $-\frac{\pi}{4}$: add $2\pi$ to get $\frac{7\pi}{4}$, the same point as the previous case, so quadrant **IV**.

**7.** Locate each angle's quadrant and apply ASTC:

* $\sin 200^\circ$: $200^\circ$ is between $180^\circ$ and $270^\circ$, quadrant III, where sine is **negative**.
* $\cos 100^\circ$: quadrant II, where cosine is **negative**.
* $\tan 250^\circ$: quadrant III, where tangent is **positive** (both coordinates negative, quotient positive).
* $\cos 340^\circ$: quadrant IV, where cosine is **positive**.

**8.** The reference angle is the acute angle to the nearest horizontal axis:

* $150^\circ$: quadrant II, $180^\circ - 150^\circ = 30^\circ$;
* $240^\circ$: quadrant III, $240^\circ - 180^\circ = 60^\circ$;
* $315^\circ$: quadrant IV, $360^\circ - 315^\circ = 45^\circ$;
* $120^\circ$: quadrant II, $180^\circ - 120^\circ = 60^\circ$.

**9.** $5\pi/6$ lies in quadrant II with reference angle $\pi - 5\pi/6 = \pi/6$. In quadrant II sine is positive and cosine and tangent are negative:

$$\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \cos\frac{5\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad \tan\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{1/2}{-\sqrt3/2} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{3}.$$

*Check:* $\tan$ is negative, as it must be in quadrant II, and its magnitude $\frac{1}{\sqrt3}$ matches $\tan\frac{\pi}{6}$.

**10.** $4\pi/3$ lies in quadrant III with reference angle $4\pi/3 - \pi = \pi/3$. In quadrant III sine and cosine are negative and tangent positive:

$$\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad \cos\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}, \qquad \tan\frac{4\pi}{3} = \frac{-\sqrt3/2}{-1/2} = \sqrt3.$$

*Check:* $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = \frac34 + \frac14 = 1$.

**11.** $7\pi/4$ lies in quadrant IV with reference angle $2\pi - 7\pi/4 = \pi/4$. In quadrant IV cosine is positive, sine and tangent negative:

$$\sin\frac{7\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}, \qquad \cos\frac{7\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}, \qquad \tan\frac{7\pi}{4} = -1.$$

*Check:* the angle is $45^\circ$ short of a full turn, so the values are those of $45^\circ$ with the sine sign reversed.

**12.** Compare with $y = A\sin(B\theta) + D$:

* $y = 4\sin\theta$: amplitude $|4| = 4$, period $\dfrac{2\pi}{1} = 2\pi$, midline $y = 0$.
* $y = -2\cos\theta + 3$: amplitude $|-2| = 2$ (the minus sign reflects the graph, it does not change the amplitude), period $\dfrac{2\pi}{1} = 2\pi$, midline $y = 3$, range $[1, 5]$.

**13.**

$$1\ \mathrm{rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.30^\circ, \qquad 1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180}\ \mathrm{rad} \approx 0.01745\ \mathrm{rad}.$$

*Check:* the two numbers are reciprocals: $57.2958 \times 0.0174533 = 1.0000$.

**14.** Rolling without slipping means the distance travelled equals the arc length rolled out:

$$s = r\theta = 0.35 \times 5 = 1.75\ \mathrm{m}.$$

The angle is given in radians, so no conversion factor is needed; had it been $5^\circ$, the distance would be $0.35 \cdot 5\pi/180 \approx 0.031\ \mathrm{m}$.

**15.** Strategy: use the area formula solved for $\theta$, then the arc-length formula.

$$A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 27 = \tfrac12 \cdot 81 \cdot \theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = \frac{54}{81} = \frac{2}{3}\ \mathrm{rad}.$$

Then

$$s = r\theta = 9 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 6\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

*Check:* $\theta = \frac23 \approx 0.667$ rad $\approx 38.2^\circ$, a modest angle, and $s = 6\ \mathrm{cm}$ is indeed smaller than the radius-times-$2$ scale of the circle. Also $\frac12 r s = \frac12 \cdot 9 \cdot 6 = 27$, consistent with $A = \frac12 r s$.

**16.** The angle comes from $s = r\theta$:

$$\theta = \frac{s}{r} = \frac{12}{8} = 1.5\ \mathrm{rad}.$$

In degrees:

$$\theta = 1.5 \cdot \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} = \frac{270^\circ}{\pi} \approx 85.94^\circ.$$

The area:

$$A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta = \tfrac12 \cdot 64 \cdot 1.5 = 48\ \mathrm{cm}^2.$$

*Check:* $A = \frac12 r s = \frac12 \cdot 8 \cdot 12 = 48$, the equivalent "half base times height" form, which must agree.

**17.** Evaluate each term by reference angle and sign:

$$\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = +\frac{\sqrt3}{2} \ (\text{II}), \qquad \cos\frac{5\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2} \ (\text{II}), \qquad \tan\frac{7\pi}{6} = +\frac{\sqrt3}{3} \ (\text{III}).$$

The sum is

$$\frac{\sqrt3}{2} - \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\sqrt3}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{3}.$$

The first two terms cancel exactly; a common error is to add their magnitudes and get $\sqrt3 + \frac{\sqrt3}{3}$.

**18.** $5\pi/4$ lies in quadrant III, since $\pi < \frac{5\pi}{4} < \frac{3\pi}{2}$. Its reference angle is

$$\alpha = \frac{5\pi}{4} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{4}.$$

In quadrant III both coordinates are negative, so

$$\sin\frac{5\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}, \qquad \cos\frac{5\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}, \qquad \tan\frac{5\pi}{4} = \frac{-\sqrt2/2}{-\sqrt2/2} = 1.$$

*Check:* $\tan$ is positive in quadrant III, and the exact value $1$ agrees with the graph of $\tan$ crossing $1$ at $\pi/4 + k\pi$, which includes $5\pi/4$.

**19.** The reference angle with sine $\frac12$ is $\frac{\pi}{6}$. Sine is positive in quadrants I and II, so

$$\theta = \frac{\pi}{6} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}.$$

*Check.* $\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$ and $\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac12$; both are in $[0, 2\pi)$, and a horizontal line $y = \frac12$ meets the unit circle exactly twice, so there are no others.

**20.** The reference angle with cosine $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ is $\frac{\pi}{6}$. Cosine is negative in quadrants II and III, so

$$\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \theta = \pi + \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{7\pi}{6}.$$

*Check.* $\cos\frac{5\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $\cos\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$.

**21.** The reference angle with $\tan\alpha = 1$ is $\frac{\pi}{4}$. Tangent is negative in quadrants II and IV, so

$$\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \theta = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{7\pi}{4}.$$

*Check.* $\tan\frac{3\pi}{4} = -1$ (quadrant II) and $\tan\frac{7\pi}{4} = -\tan\frac{\pi}{4} = -1$ (quadrant IV).

* period $= \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$;
* zeros where $3\theta = k\pi$, that is $\theta = \dfrac{k\pi}{3}$. In $[0, 2\pi)$ these are

Within one period of length $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ there are exactly two zeros, consistent with the two zeros $0, \frac{\pi}{3}$ and the repeat at $\frac{2\pi}{3}$.

At $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$ (half period): $4\theta = \pi$, so $y = -6$, the minimum. The range is $[-6, 4]$, consistent with amplitude $5$ about the midline $-1$.

**25.** The seat starts at the lowest point, so the model is a cosine reflected about the centre: the height is the centre height minus the radius times $\cos$, which starts at $-R$ when $t = 0$.

## Advanced

**26.** Let $A = (1,0)$ be the point where the circle meets the positive $x$-axis, $P = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ with $0 < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$, and $T = (1, \tan\theta)$ the point where the tangent at $A$ meets the ray $OP$.

All three regions have the same two bounding rays $OA$ and $OP$ between them, and they are nested:

* **Triangle $OAP$.** Its base is $OA = 1$ and its height is the $y$-coordinate of $P$, namely $\sin\theta$. So its area is $\tfrac12 \sin\theta$.
* **Sector $OAP$.** By Property 8.3 with $r = 1$, its area is $\tfrac12 \theta$.
* **Triangle $OAT$.** Its base is $OA = 1$ and its height is the $y$-coordinate of $T$, namely $\tan\theta$. So its area is $\tfrac12\tan\theta$.

Since triangle $OAP$ lies inside the sector (the chord $AP$ lies inside the arc) and the sector lies inside triangle $OAT$ (the arc lies inside the tangent line),

$$\tfrac12\sin\theta < \tfrac12\theta < \tfrac12\tan\theta.$$

Multiply by $2$:

$$\sin\theta < \theta < \tan\theta.$$

Now divide the whole chain by the positive number $\sin\theta$ (positive because $0 < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$):

$$1 < \frac{\theta}{\sin\theta} < \frac{1}{\cos\theta}.$$

As $\theta \to 0^+$, $\cos\theta \to 1$, so the outer bounds both tend to $1$. By the squeeze principle, $\dfrac{\theta}{\sin\theta} \to 1$, hence $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \to 1$. This is the precise meaning of $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ for small $\theta$.

*Note.* The comparison of a length with an angle is legitimate only because the circle has radius $1$: the arc length of the sector equals $\theta$ exactly, so $\theta$ *is* a length here. In degrees the same arc would have length $\frac{\pi}{180}\theta$, and the chain would read $\sin\theta < \frac{\pi\theta}{180} < \tan\theta$.

**27.** Let $P = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.

Reflect $P$ in the $y$-axis. The reflection sends $(x, y)$ to $(-x, y)$. The reflected point is on the unit circle, and its angle is measured from the positive $x$-axis to the reflected ray; the ray makes the same angle with the negative $x$-axis as the original makes with the positive one, so its angle is $\pi - \theta$. Therefore

$$P(\pi - \theta) = (-\cos\theta,\, \sin\theta).$$

Reading the coordinates:

$$\cos(\pi - \theta) = -\cos\theta, \qquad \sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta. \qquad \blacksquare$$

The geometric content is that reflection in the $y$-axis reverses the horizontal coordinate and preserves the vertical one; in trigonometric language, cosine changes sign and sine does not.

**28.** **(a)** By Definition 5.5 and the half-turn relation (Property 8.6),

$$\tan(\theta + \pi) = \frac{\sin(\theta+\pi)}{\cos(\theta+\pi)} = \frac{-\sin\theta}{-\cos\theta} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta,$$

wherever $\cos\theta \ne 0$; the equation $\cos(\theta + \pi) = -\cos\theta$ shows both sides are defined at the same points.

**(b)** Suppose $\tan(\theta + p) = \tan\theta$ for all $\theta$ at which both sides are defined, with $p > 0$. Take $\theta = 0$, which is admissible because $\tan 0 = 0$ is defined:

$$\tan p = \tan 0 = 0.$$

A tangent is zero exactly where its sine is zero, that is at integer multiples of $\pi$. Hence $p = k\pi$ for some integer $k$, and since $p > 0$ we get $p \ge \pi$. By part (a), $p = \pi$ does satisfy the equation for all $\theta$. Therefore the least positive period is exactly $\pi$.

*Contrast with sine.* The same argument for sine fails at the first step: $\sin p = \sin 0 = 0$ gives only $p = k\pi$, and whereas $p = \pi$ satisfies $\sin(\theta+\pi) = -\sin\theta \ne \sin\theta$ in general, one more test is needed. Taking $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ gives $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + p\right) = \cos p$, which must equal $\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$; hence $p$ is a multiple of $2\pi$. This is the subject of Problem 33.

**29.** Write $T(t) = 15 - 10\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6}\right)$, so $A = -10$, $B = \frac{\pi}{6}$, $D = 15$.

* Period: $\dfrac{2\pi}{\pi/6} = 12$ months, as expected for an annual cycle.
* Maximum: $D + |A| = 15 + 10 = 25\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, attained when $\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6}\right) = -1$, i.e. $\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6} = \pi + 2k\pi$, so $t - 1 = 6 + 12k$ and $t = 7$ in the first year: July.
* Minimum: $15 - 10 = 5\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, when $\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6}\right) = 1$, i.e. $t = 1$: January.

For $T(t) \ge 20$:

$$15 - 10\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6}\right) \ge 20 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -10\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6}\right) \ge 5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6}\right) \le -\frac{1}{2}.$$

With $u = \frac{\pi(t-1)}{6}$, the inequality $\cos u \le -\frac12$ holds for $u \in \left[\frac{2\pi}{3}, \frac{4\pi}{3}\right]$ modulo $2\pi$. Hence

$$\frac{2\pi}{3} \le \frac{\pi(t-1)}{6} \le \frac{4\pi}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4 \le t - 1 \le 8 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 5 \le t \le 9.$$

So the temperature reaches at least $20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ from May to September inclusive — five months, symmetric about the July maximum, as a cosine must be.

*Check.* At $t = 5$ and $t = 9$: $\frac{\pi(t-1)}{6} = \frac{4\pi}{6} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$ and $\frac{8\pi}{6} = \frac{4\pi}{3}$; the cosine is $-\frac12$ at both, giving $T = 15 + 5 = 20$. ✓

**30.** **(a)** Set $D(t) = 3$:

**31.** Let the chord $AB$ subtend the central angle $\theta$ at the centre $O$, so $OA = OB = r$. Triangle $OAB$ is isosceles. Drop the perpendicular from $O$ to the chord, meeting it at $M$. In an isosceles triangle the altitude from the apex bisects both the apex angle and the base, so

$$\angle AOM = \frac{\theta}{2}, \qquad AM = MB = \frac{c}{2}.$$

In the right triangle $AOM$, the hypotenuse is $r$ and the side opposite the angle $\frac{\theta}{2}$ is $\frac{c}{2}$:

$$\sin\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{c/2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c = 2r\sin\frac{\theta}{2}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

For $r = 10$ and $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$:

$$c = 20\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = 20 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 10\sqrt3 \approx 17.32.$$

*Check.* The chord is less than the diameter $20$ and, since $\frac{2\pi}{3} > \frac{\pi}{2}$, it should be longer than the side of an inscribed square, $r\sqrt2 \approx 14.14$; indeed $17.32 > 14.14$.

**32.** Convert first: $3^\circ = 3 \cdot \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{60} \approx 0.05235988$ rad.

The small-angle approximation gives

$$\sin 3^\circ \approx 0.05235988,$$

while the true value is $\sin 3^\circ \approx 0.05233596$. The absolute error is

$$0.05235988 - 0.05233596 = 2.392 \times 10^{-5},$$

and the relative error is

$$\frac{2.392\times10^{-5}}{0.05233596} \approx 4.57\times10^{-4} = 0.0457\%.$$

The prediction $\dfrac{\theta^2}{6}$ is

$$\frac{(0.05235988)^2}{6} = \frac{0.00274155}{6} \approx 4.569\times10^{-4} = 0.0457\%,$$

which agrees to the accuracy shown. So $\sin 3^\circ \approx 0.05236$ is accurate to better than one part in two thousand — far better than most instruments can measure an angle.

## Challenge

**33.** **Existence.** $\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta$ for all $\theta$, since adding a full turn returns $P(\theta)$ to itself; so $2\pi$ is a period.

**Minimality.** Suppose $p > 0$ satisfies $\sin(\theta + p) = \sin\theta$ for every real $\theta$.

*Setting $\theta = 0$* gives $\sin p = \sin 0 = 0$. The sine is zero exactly at integer multiples of $\pi$, so $p = k\pi$ for some positive integer $k$.
*Setting $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$* gives $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + p\right) = \sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$. Now $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + p\right) = \cos p$ by the complementary relation, so $\cos p = 1$. A cosine equals $1$ only at integer multiples of $2\pi$, so $p = 2m\pi$ for some positive integer $m$.

The least positive value of $2m\pi$ is $2\pi$, hence the fundamental period is $2\pi$. $\blacksquare$

**Why $\cos\theta = \sin\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.** Rotating the plane by a quarter turn counterclockwise about the origin sends the basis point $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$ and $(0,1)$ to $(-1,0)$; rotations preserve lengths and angles, so the unit circle maps to itself and the point at angle $\theta$ goes to the point at angle $\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}$. On coordinates the quarter-turn acts as

$$(x, y) \longmapsto (-y, x).$$

Applying this to $P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$,

$$P\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = (-\sin\theta,\, \cos\theta).$$

Reading off the second coordinate gives $\sin\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \cos\theta$. (The first coordinate gives the companion relation $\cos\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = -\sin\theta$.)

*Consistency check with the reflection proofs.* The complementary relation $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \cos\theta$ (reflection in $y = x$) and the supplementary relation $\sin(\pi - u) = \sin u$ with $u = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$ together give $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \theta\right) = \cos\theta$ as well. The two derivations agree, as they must.

Around each of the two adjacent corners: $90^\circ$ of radius $15$.** When the rope is pulled tight past an adjacent corner, $10\ \mathrm{m}$ of it lies along the wall, leaving $25 - 10 = 15\ \mathrm{m}$ free. On the far side of that wall the free rope can sweep a quarter-turn before the next wall stops it. There are two such corners, and their sectors do not overlap (they lie on opposite sides of the shed):

$$A_2 = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{4}\pi (15)^2 = 2 \cdot \frac{225\pi}{4} = 112.5\pi.$$

**3. Around the far corner: $90^\circ$ of radius $5$.** Reaching the corner diagonally opposite $O$ consumes two wall lengths, $10 + 10 = 20\ \mathrm{m}$, leaving $5\ \mathrm{m}$. The free end then sweeps a further quarter-turn behind the shed:

$$A_3 = \frac{1}{4}\pi (5)^2 = \frac{25\pi}{4} = 6.25\pi.$$

**Total.**

$$A = 468.75\pi + 112.5\pi + 6.25\pi = 587.5\pi \approx 1845.7\ \mathrm{m}^2.$$

*Justification of each sector.* Each is bounded by two straight walls (or by a wall and the taut rope) meeting at a right angle, and the rope pivots about the corner, so the reachable set is exactly the set of points within the remaining radius in that right-angled corner — a quarter-disc. The three parts are disjoint because each lies in a different region cut out by the shed's walls and their extensions, and the far-corner disc lies beyond the near ones. Adding their areas therefore does not double-count.

*Sanity check.* Ignoring the shed entirely, the goat would graze $\pi(25)^2 = 625\pi \approx 1963.5\ \mathrm{m}^2$. The shed removes a quarter-disc of radius $25$ ($156.25\pi$) but the rope wrapping restores $118.75\pi$ of that on the far side, giving $587.5\pi < 625\pi$. Consistent.

**35.** **Setup.** Let the chord $AB$ cut off a segment, with central angle $\angle AOB = \theta$ and $OA = OB = r$. The segment is exactly the sector $AOB$ minus the triangle $AOB$.

**Sector.** By Property 8.3,

$$A_{\text{sector}} = \tfrac12 r^2\theta.$$

**Triangle.** Take $OA$ as the base, of length $r$. The height of the triangle relative to this base is the perpendicular distance from $B$ to the line $OA$, which is $r\sin\theta$ (the vertical coordinate of $B$ when $A$ is placed on the positive $x$-axis). Therefore

$$A_{\text{triangle}} = \tfrac12 \cdot r \cdot r\sin\theta = \tfrac12 r^2\sin\theta.$$

This uses only the right-triangle definition of sine, with the altitude dropped from $B$.

**Segment.**

$$A_{\text{segment}} = A_{\text{sector}} - A_{\text{triangle}} = \tfrac12 r^2\theta - \tfrac12 r^2\sin\theta = \tfrac12 r^2(\theta - \sin\theta). \qquad \blacksquare$$

Because $\sin\theta < \theta$ for $0 < \theta < \pi$, the difference is positive, as an area must be.

**Evaluation** for $r = 10\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$:

$$A = \tfrac12 \cdot 100 \cdot \left(\frac{\pi}{3} - \sin\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = 50\left(\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) \approx 50(1.0471976 - 0.8660254) = 50 \times 0.1811722 \approx 9.06\ \mathrm{cm}^2.$$

*Check.* The sector has area $\frac12 \cdot 100 \cdot \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{50\pi}{3} \approx 52.36\ \mathrm{cm}^2$ and the triangle $\frac12 \cdot 100 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 25\sqrt3 \approx 43.30\ \mathrm{cm}^2$; their difference $9.06$ is small compared with either, which is right because a $60^\circ$ chord cuts off a shallow cap.

**36.** **(a) Fitting the model.** Let $D(t) = D + A\cos\left(B(t - t_0)\right)$.

* **Midline** — the average of high and low water: $D = \dfrac{8.0 + 2.0}{2} = 5.0\ \mathrm{m}$.
* **Amplitude** — half the range: $A = \dfrac{8.0 - 2.0}{2} = 3.0\ \mathrm{m}$.
* **Period** — high water repeats every $12$ hours, so $\dfrac{2\pi}{B} = 12$, giving $B = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$.
* **Phase** — the maximum is at $t_0 = 4$ (04:00), and the cosine attains its maximum at argument zero, so $t_0 = 4$.

$$D(t) = 5 + 3\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t - 4)}{6}\right).$$

**(b) Predictions.**

* $t = 0$: $\dfrac{\pi(0-4)}{6} = -\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$, and $\cos\left(-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac12$, so $D(0) = 5 - 1.5 = 3.5\ \mathrm{m}$.
* $t = 7$: $\dfrac{\pi \cdot 3}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and $\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$, so $D(7) = 5.0\ \mathrm{m}$.
* $t = 13$: $\dfrac{\pi \cdot 9}{6} = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$, and $\cos\frac{3\pi}{2} = 0$, so $D(13) = 5.0\ \mathrm{m}$.

The last two are the mid-tide levels, exactly halfway in time between high and low water — as a cosine must give.

**(c) The window with $D \ge 6.0$.**

$$5 + 3\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-4)}{6}\right) \ge 6 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-4)}{6}\right) \ge \frac{1}{3}.$$

The equation $\cos u = \frac13$ has $u = \arccos\left(\frac13\right) \approx 1.23096$ rad, and cosine is at least $\frac13$ for $|u| \le 1.23096$. Hence

$$\left|t - 4\right| \le \frac{6}{\pi} \times 1.23096 \approx 2.351.$$

So $D \ge 6.0\ \mathrm{m}$ for

$$1.649 \le t \le 6.351,$$

that is from about **01:39 to 06:21**, a window of length $2 \times 2.351 \approx 4.70$ hours. The same window repeats $12$ hours later, from about 13:39 to 18:21.

**(d) Two idealisations.**

1. **A single sinusoid is too simple.** Real tides are sums of many constituents — the lunar and solar semidiurnal terms alone have different periods and amplitudes — so successive high waters differ in height over a spring–neap cycle of about two weeks, and shallow-water effects distort the curve away from a pure cosine. A tide table therefore lists corrections, not just one amplitude and phase.
2. **The period is not exactly $12$ hours.** The dominant semidiurnal lunar constituent (M2) has a period of about $12.42$ hours, not $12$. With a $12$-hour model the predicted high water drifts earlier by about $0.42$ h per cycle, roughly $50$ minutes per day, so the model is useful for one cycle or two but not for a week.

A third assumption worth naming is that the depth measured at the reference gauge is the depth at the vessel's berth; in a real harbour the two differ because of the shape of the basin and river inflow.
